\section{Introduction}
% =====================================================================

In a penalty kick, one player shoots at a goal defended only by the
goalkeeper. The ball starts on a mark \SI{11}{\metre} in front of a goal
\SI{7.32}{\metre} wide and \SI{2.44}{\metre} high \citep{ifab2025}.
A well-struck penalty covers that distance in about \SI{0.4}{\second}, too
fast for the goalkeeper to wait and see where the ball goes, as human visual
reaction time alone accounts for $\approx\SI{0.2}{\second}$ before physical
movement even begins \citep{teichner1954}. To block the
kick, the goalkeeper has to anticipate the trajectory from the first
instants of the ball's flight. This paper asks how well that can be done:
given the ball's position at two moments shortly after the kick, where will
it most likely cross the goal line, and how sure can we be?

The difficulty is that the first instants say little about the forces that
decide where the ball ends up. Over the first \SI{0.1}{\second} the ball
travels about \SI{3}{\metre}, and the curve that spin puts into its path
is only about \SI{1}{\centi\metre}, the same size as the precision of the
positions we are given. By the time the ball reaches the goal, the same spin
has moved it by tens of centimeters. Drag, which slows the ball, depends on
its speed and spin in ways that cannot be measured from two positions.
Predicting the crossing point is therefore not a curve-fitting problem but
an extrapolation problem, and an honest answer has to say how uncertain the
extrapolation is.

Our approach combines a physical model of the ball's flight with a
statistical treatment of what the observations cannot determine.
Section~\ref{sec:restatement} restates the problem and our interpretation
of it. Section~\ref{sec:simple-model} introduces the forces on the ball and
the assumptions of a simplified model. Section~\ref{sec:exact-fits} applies
that model in the case where the problem is well constrained, and finds the
trajectories that pass through the given positions to within their rounding.
Section~\ref{sec:montecarlo} extends this to a Monte Carlo method that samples
the quantities the data cannot fix, from distributions based on measured kicks,
and weights each sample by how well it explains the observations.
Section~\ref{sec:results} gives the predicted crossing point and its
uncertainty for the given scenario, together with tests of the method on
simulated kicks, and Section~\ref{sec:discussion} discusses the strengths
and weaknesses of the approach.

\section{Problem Restatement}
\label{sec:restatement}
% =====================================================================

A penalty kick is taken from the penalty mark. We use the coordinate system
of the problem, in meters:
\begin{itemize}
  \item the origin is at the penalty mark;
  \item the $x$-axis points toward the goal, which lies in the plane $x = 11$;
  \item the $y$-axis runs across the goal, with $+y$ on the kicker's left;
  \item the $z$-axis points up.
\end{itemize}
The goal mouth is the region $-3.66 < y < 3.66$, $0 < z < 2.44$ of the
plane $x = 11$. At $t = 0$ the center of the ball is at
$\bm{r}_0 = (0, 0, 0.11)$, one ball radius above the mark. The position of
the ball is then observed at $t_1 = \SI{0.05}{\second}$ and
$t_2 = \SI{0.10}{\second}$.

The problem asks us to develop a general method that, given the coordinates
of the ball at $t_1$ and $t_2$,
\begin{enumerate}
  \item predicts the point at which the ball is most likely to enter the goal, and
  \item estimates the uncertainty of that prediction.
\end{enumerate}
We are to test the method and apply it to the scenario in
Table~\ref{tab:scenario}.

  \centering

    The position at $t = 0$ is given exactly.}
  \label{tab:scenario}
  \begin{tabular}{S[table-format=1.2] S[table-format=1.2] S[table-format=1.2] S[table-format=1.2]}
    \toprule
    {$t$ (\si{\second})} & {$x$ (\si{\metre})} & {$y$ (\si{\metre})} & {$z$ (\si{\metre})} \\

  \end{tabular}
\end{table}

We interpret the problem as follows.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{A general method.} The method takes any two observed
    positions as input. Nothing in it is tuned to the given scenario,
    which serves as one application of it.
  \item \textbf{Coordinates of the ball.} The problem gives the position
    at $t = 0$ as the center of the ball, so we take the observed
    coordinates at $t_1$ and $t_2$ to be positions of the center as well.
  \item \textbf{Entering the goal.} We take the point where the ball
    ``enters the goal'' to be the $(y, z)$ position of its center when the
    center reaches the goal plane $x = 11$. A shot counts as on target when
    the center crosses inside the goal mouth defined above, as \emph{wide}
    when $|y| \geq 3.66$, and as \emph{over} when $z \geq 2.44$.
  \item \textbf{Off-target shots are part of the answer.} The ball is not
    guaranteed to enter the goal, so we predict where it crosses the whole
    goal plane, inside or outside the frame. Alongside the most likely
    point we report the probability that the ball enters the goal, and the
    probabilities that it goes wide or over.
  \item \textbf{Precision of the data.} The observed positions are given
    to the nearest centimeter, so each coordinate is known only to within
    about $\pm\SI{5}{\milli\metre}$. A trajectory that passes within this
    distance of every observation is consistent with the data.
  \item \textbf{Most likely point and uncertainty.} Because the observations
    cannot determine every quantity that affects the flight, the prediction
    is a probability distribution over the goal plane. We take the most
    likely crossing point to be the peak of that distribution, and describe
    the uncertainty by its spread, by the regions that contain 68\% and 95\%
    of the probability, and by the outcome probabilities.
  \item \textbf{Testing.} We test the method both on the given scenario and
    on simulated kicks whose true crossing points are known, which checks
    whether the stated uncertainty is honest.
\end{itemize}

\section{Simplified Model and Assumptions}
\label{sec:simple-model}
% =====================================================================

We first build a deterministic model: given the ball's launch velocity, its
spin and a few physical constants, it predicts the whole flight. The method in
Sections~\ref{sec:exact-fits} and~\ref{sec:montecarlo} uses this model to work
backward from the two observed positions.

\subsection{Forces on the ball}
\label{sec:forces}

Once the ball has left the foot, three forces act on it
(Figure~\ref{fig:forces}): gravity, aerodynamic drag, and the Magnus lift that
comes from spin. Writing $\bm{r}(t)$ for the position of the ball's center and
$\bm{v} = \dot{\bm{r}}$ for its velocity,
\begin{equation}
  m\,\dot{\bm{v}} \;=\; m\bm{g} \;+\; \bm{F}_D \;+\; \bm{F}_L ,
  \label{eq:eom}
\end{equation}
with $m$ the ball's mass and $\bm{g} = (0, 0, -g)$, $g = \SI{9.81}{\metre\per\second\squared}$.

\paragraph{Drag.} Drag opposes the velocity and grows with the square of the
speed in the high-Reynolds-number regime characteristic of macroscopic balls in air \citep{timmerman1999}:
\begin{equation}
  \bm{F}_D = -\tfrac{1}{2}\rho A\, C_D\, |\bm{v}|\,\bm{v},
  \label{eq:drag}
\end{equation}
where $\rho$ is the air density, $A = \pi r^2$ is the ball's cross-section and
$C_D$ is the drag coefficient.

\paragraph{Magnus lift.} A spinning ball drags air around with it, deflecting
the flow and pushing the ball sideways to its direction of travel
\citep{kray2014}. The force
is perpendicular to both the velocity and the spin axis:
\begin{equation}
  \bm{F}_L = \tfrac{1}{2}\rho A\, C_L\, |\bm{v}|^2\,
             \frac{\bm{\omega} \times \bm{v}}{|\bm{\omega} \times \bm{v}|},
  \label{eq:lift}
\end{equation}
where $\bm{\omega}$ is the spin (angular velocity) and $C_L$ is the lift
coefficient. Backspin lifts the ball, topspin makes it dip and sidespin makes
it curl.

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.5\linewidth]{forces}
  \caption{Forces on the ball in flight: gravity $m\bm{g}$, drag $\bm{F}_D$
    opposite the velocity $\bm{v}$, and Magnus lift $\bm{F}_L$ perpendicular to
    both $\bm{v}$ and the spin $\bm{\omega}$. Here $\bm{\omega}$ points out of
    the page, which for a ball moving to the right is backspin, so the lift
    points up.}
  \label{fig:forces}
\end{figure}

Dividing Equation~\eqref{eq:eom} by $m$ gives the equations we integrate,
\begin{equation}
  \dot{\bm{r}} = \bm{v}, \qquad
  \dot{\bm{v}} = \bm{g} \;-\; k\, C_D\, |\bm{v}|\,\bm{v}
                 \;+\; k\, C_L\, |\bm{v}|^2\, \hat{\bm{n}},
  \qquad k = \frac{\rho A}{2m},
  \label{eq:accel}
\end{equation}
with $\hat{\bm{n}}$ the unit vector along $\bm{\omega}\times\bm{v}$. For a
regulation ball ($m = \SI{0.43}{\kilo\gram}$, $r = \SI{0.11}{\metre}$) in air
of density \SI{1.20}{\kilo\gram\per\metre\cubed}, $k = \SI{0.053}{\per\metre}$.
At \SI{32}{\metre\per\second}, the launch speed we fit in
Section~\ref{sec:exact-fits}, drag decelerates a
non-spinning ball by about \SI{8}{\metre\per\second\squared}. A spin of
\SI{9.3}{\rev\per\second}, at the top of the range measured when players' kicks are filmed with
high-speed cameras (``measured kicks'' below; \citealp{tsukada2011}), gives a lift of about
\SI{16}{\metre\per\second\squared}. Both are comparable
to gravity, so none of the three forces can be left out.

\subsection{Drag and lift coefficients}
\label{sec:coefficients}

The aerodynamic coefficients $C_D$ and $C_L$ are not constants. They depend
dynamically on the ball's instantaneous speed and on the ratio of surface
rotational speed to translational velocity, characterized by the non-dimensional
spin parameter. Because only the component of angular velocity orthogonal to the
flight path deflects the oncoming airflow, we define the \emph{perpendicular}
spin parameter:
\begin{equation}
  S_\perp = \frac{r\,|\bm{\omega}\times\bm{v}|}{|\bm{v}|^2}
          = \frac{r\,|\bm{\omega}|\sin\theta}{|\bm{v}|},
  \label{eq:sperp}
\end{equation}
where $\theta$ represents the angle between the spin axis and the velocity. Spin
directed strictly along the trajectory (``rifle'' or screw spin, $\theta = 0$)
generates no cross-stream pressure imbalance and therefore no Magnus deflection.
Furthermore, extensive wind-tunnel investigations spanning both smooth spheres
and panelled soccer balls confirm that cross-flow aerodynamic forces are
governed by the perpendicular component, with negligible lateral cross-coupling
from axial rotation \citep{kray2014}. Because the soccer ball decelerates due
to drag and curves under lift, both $S_\perp$ and the resulting force
coefficients are evaluated dynamically at each numerical integration step.

\paragraph{Parameterization via Kiratidis \& Leinweber (2018).}
We parameterize both coefficients using the empirical models of
\citet{kiratidis2018}, who fitted experimental datasets across modern FIFA match
balls, including the wind-tunnel measurements of \citet{asai2007} and the
high-speed trajectory tracking data of \citet{goff2010}. Specifically, we adopt
their parameters calibrated for the thermally bonded 14-panel Adidas Teamgeist.

For a non-spinning ball, the drag coefficient exhibits a classic boundary-layer
transition (\emph{the drag crisis}) near \SI{13}{\metre\per\second}, followed by
a flat turbulent plateau across game-speed regimes \citep[Eq.~5]{kiratidis2018}:
\begin{equation}
  C_{D0}(v) = \frac{a - b_{\min}}{1 + e^{(v - v_c)/v_s}} + b_{\min}
  + \frac{v - v_{\min}}{1 + e^{-(v - v_{\min})/v_s}}\,
    \frac{b_{\max} - b_{\min}}{v_{\max} - v_{\min}},
  \label{eq:cd0}
\end{equation}
with $v = |\bm{v}|$, subcritical drag $a = 0.4927$, critical speed
$v_c = \SI{12.58}{\metre\per\second}$, transition width
$v_s = \SI{1.071}{\metre\per\second}$, minimum post-crisis drag
$b_{\min} = 0.1440$, plateau slope bound $b_{\max} = 0.1540$, and speed interval
$[v_{\min}, v_{\max}] = [\SI{23.17}{}, \SI{35.00}{}]\,\si{\metre\per\second}$.

Boundary-layer rotation introduces an additional transverse drag penalty that
scales linearly with $S_\perp$ \citep[Eq.~7]{kiratidis2018}:
\begin{equation}
  C_D(v, S_\perp) = C_{D0}(v) + 0.514\, S_\perp .
  \label{eq:cd}
\end{equation}

The lift coefficient follows a sub-linear power law in $S_\perp$, attenuated if
drag approaches subcritical baseline levels \citep[Eqs.~8--9]{kiratidis2018}:
\begin{equation}
  C_L(v, S_\perp) = 1.15\, S_\perp^{0.83}\;
  \frac{C_{D0}(0) - \min\!\big(C_D(v, S_\perp),\, C_{D0}(0)\big)}
       {C_{D0}(0) - C_{D,\mathrm{ref}}},
  \label{eq:cl}
\end{equation}
where $C_{D0}(0) \approx 0.493$ denotes the subcritical laminar limit and
$C_{D,\mathrm{ref}} \approx 0.242$ is the calibration drag at the reference
anchor point (Reynolds number $3.34\times10^5$, $S_\perp = 0.19$, corresponding
to $v_{\mathrm{ref}} \approx \SI{23.6}{\metre\per\second}$ for kinematic
viscosity $\nu = \SI{1.54e-5}{\metre\squared\per\second}$ and diameter
$D = \SI{0.218}{\metre}$; \citealp{goff2010}). These constants are the calibration
of \citet{kiratidis2018}, who matched the wind-tunnel data of \citet{asai2007} at
this Reynolds number: $C_L = 0.29$ at $S_\perp = 0.19$ and $C_L = 0.33$ at
$S_\perp = 0.31$. The same curve gives $C_L = 0.14$ at $S_\perp = 0.06$, against a
measured 0.15.

It is important to note that \citet{kiratidis2018} did not report experimental
uncertainties or error bounds for their digitized wind-tunnel datasets and
trajectory fits (shown in Figure~\ref{fig:coefficients}). As they noted,
empirical measurements of sports balls exhibit considerable trial-to-trial
scatter due to unpredictable seam orientations relative to the airflow and
subtle boundary-layer tripping during launch, yet no formal confidence intervals
or error bars accompany their equations. This absence of published uncertainty
bounds directly motivates the sensitivity analyses and Monte Carlo sampling
framework developed in Section~\ref{sec:montecarlo}.

\paragraph{Cross-validation with Kray et al. (2014) and simulation bounds.}
To verify whether these formulations and our simulation bounds represent
physically realistic aerodynamics rather than isolated model artifacts, we
compare them against the independent wind-tunnel measurements of
\citet{kray2014}, who tested an identical 14-panel Teamgeist geometry mounted
on an internal drive sting across $0.96\times 10^5 \le \mathrm{Re} \le 4.62\times 10^5$
and $0 \le S_\perp \le 1.1$:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Supercritical drag baseline ($C_D$):} In our simulation engine,
    penalty kicks launch between \SI{20}{} and \SI{34}{\metre\per\second}
    ($\mathrm{Re} \approx 2.8\times 10^5\text{ to }4.9\times 10^5$). Across this
    entire window, Equation~\eqref{eq:cd0} yields a virtually flat non-spinning
    baseline of $C_{D0} \approx 0.144\text{--}0.154$. \citet{kray2014} report
    supercritical drag coefficients of $C_D \approx 0.15\text{--}0.24$ in the
    post-critical plateau ($\mathrm{Re} > 2.5\times 10^5$), corroborating our
    baseline magnitude.
  \item \textbf{Spin-induced drag increment:} Equation~\eqref{eq:cd} adds
    $\Delta C_D = 0.514\,S_\perp$, predicting an increase of
    $\Delta C_D \approx 0.15$ at maximum realistic penalty spin rates
    ($S_\perp \approx 0.30$, corresponding to \SI{8}{\rev\per\second}). This
    closely mirrors \citet[Fig.~9]{kray2014}, where at
    $\mathrm{Re} = 3.42\times 10^5$, drag rises from $C_D \approx 0.20$ at
    $S_\perp = 0$ to $C_D \approx 0.35$ at $S_\perp = 0.30$.
  \item \textbf{Lift saturation ceiling ($C_L$):} Equation~\eqref{eq:cl} yields
    $C_L \approx 0.33$ at $S_\perp \approx 0.30$. \citet{kray2014} demonstrate
    that for supercritical Reynolds numbers, the Magnus coefficient levels off
    into a plateau between $0.30 < C_M < 0.35$. The Monte Carlo model of
    Section~\ref{sec:montecarlo} also lets the lift coefficient fall by up to
    \SI{25}{\percent} to cover other balls (Section~\ref{sec:priors}).
  \item \textbf{Regime consistency and anomalous effects:} While \citet{kray2014}
    documented a ``negative Magnus effect'' ($C_M \approx -0.09$) caused by
    asymmetric boundary-layer laminarization, they showed that this inversion
    is strictly confined to the critical transition regime
    ($\mathrm{Re} \approx 2.06\times 10^5$) at low spin ($S_\perp \approx 0.12$).
    Because high-speed penalty kicks operate at $\mathrm{Re} > 3.0\times 10^5$,
    the boundary layers on both sides remain fully turbulent, ensuring that the
    Magnus force operates exclusively in the classical positive regime
    ($C_L > 0$). Furthermore, \citet{kray2014} observed that below rotational
    frequencies of \SI{2}{\hertz} ($S_\perp < 0.1$), coherent Magnus circulation
    gives way to erratic lateral force fluctuations (``knuckling''). This is
    why we treat knuckling as a separate force that acts only at low spin
    (Section~\ref{sec:variants}).
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Spin Decay}
\label{sec:spin-decay}

Air friction slows the ball's spin during flight. \citet{tsukada2008} filmed
22 kicks at \SIrange{19}{31}{\metre\per\second} and found that they lost
\SIrange{5}{15}{\percent} of their spin over \SI{18}{\metre}, independent of
the initial spin, with the direction of the spin axis unchanged. We model this
as an exponential decay with the distance $s$ the ball has travelled,
\begin{equation}
  |\bm{\omega}(t)| = |\bm{\omega}_0|\, e^{-s(t)/L}, \qquad \dot{s} = |\bm{v}|,
  \label{eq:spindecay}
\end{equation}
with the axis fixed. This is the form that follows from an aerodynamic
spin-down torque proportional to spin times speed, which
\citet{james2008} measured for a football. The measured losses correspond to a decay
length $L$ between \SI{110}{\metre} and \SI{350}{\metre}. Over the
\SI{11}{\metre} of a penalty the spin therefore falls by only
\SIrange{3}{10}{\percent}.

\subsection{Solving the Equations}
\label{sec:integration}

Equations~\eqref{eq:accel}--\eqref{eq:spindecay} have no closed-form solution,
so we integrate them numerically with the adaptive eighth-order Runge--Kutta
method DOP853 \citep{hairer1993}, as implemented in SciPy \citep{scipy2020}.
The state is the position, velocity and distance travelled. The integration
starts at $\bm{r}_0 = (0, 0, 0.11)$ with launch velocity $\bm{v}_0$ and stops
when the ball's center reaches the goal plane $x = 11$, which the solver
locates as an event.

The method has no fixed time step. At each step it estimates its own error
and chooses the next step so that the error stays below a set tolerance. We
use a relative tolerance of $10^{-9}$ and an absolute tolerance of
$10^{-11}$ for every result we report, and the looser $10^{-7}$ and $10^{-9}$
inside the fitting loops of Section~\ref{sec:search}, where many trajectories
are integrated. Because the forces vary smoothly, the solver needs only four
or five steps to reach the goal: a first step of about \SI{0.4}{\milli\second},
then steps of up to about \SI{0.3}{\second}. Positions at the observation
times and at the goal plane come from the method's built-in high-order
interpolation between steps \citep{hairer1993}. To confirm that these long
steps are accurate, we compared trajectories with a reference integration at
a relative tolerance of $10^{-13}$. The crossing points differ by less than
\SI{1}{\nano\metre} at our standard tolerance and by less than
\SI{2}{\micro\metre} at the looser one, far below any other source of error.

We also checked the solver against cases with exact solutions: flight under
gravity alone, drag alone in one dimension, and lift alone, for which the ball
moves in a circle at constant speed. In each case the numerical and exact
trajectories agree to better than \SI{1e-6}{\metre}.

\subsection{Assumptions}
\label{sec:assumptions}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{The ball is a rigid sphere of radius \SI{0.11}{\metre}.}
    The problem places the ball's center \SI{0.11}{\metre} above the ground,
    and the Laws of the Game require a circumference of
    \SIrange{68}{70}{\centi\metre}, a radius of
    \SIrange{10.8}{11.1}{\centi\metre} \citep{ifab2025}.
  \item \textbf{The ball leaves the foot at $t = 0$ from $\bm{r}_0$.} This is
    how the problem defines the kick. After $t = 0$ the foot no longer touches
    the ball.
  \item \textbf{Only gravity, drag and Magnus lift act on the ball.} Here and
    in Section~\ref{sec:exact-fits} we neglect buoyancy, which is
    \SI{1.6}{\percent} of the ball's weight, and the added mass of the air the
    ball carries with it; together they change its height at the goal by about
    \SI{1}{\centi\metre}. We also neglect the random sideways forces that act
    on balls kicked with almost no spin (``knuckling''), which are strongest
    below about \SI{1}{\rev\per\second} \citep{mizota2013}.
    Section~\ref{sec:variants} adds both effects.
  \item \textbf{The air is still.} The two observations cannot detect wind,
    so it cannot be estimated from the data. In our model, ignoring a
    crosswind of \SI{1}{\metre\per\second} moves the predicted crossing
    point by about \SI{1.3}{\centi\metre}.
  \item \textbf{The ball has the drag and lift of the Teamgeist.} Balls with
    different panel designs have different drag and lift curves
    \citep{kiratidis2018}. Here and in Section~\ref{sec:exact-fits} we use the
    Teamgeist curves; Section~\ref{sec:montecarlo} covers the other balls by
    scaling both coefficients (Section~\ref{sec:priors}).
  \item \textbf{The spin axis keeps its direction and the spin rate decays
    with distance} as in Equation~\eqref{eq:spindecay} \citep{tsukada2008}.
  \item \textbf{The flight ends at the goal plane.} We do not model the ball
    hitting a post, the crossbar or the ground. The crossing point is that of
    the ball's center, as the problem defines it.
\end{enumerate}

In this section and the next we also hold the air density, ball mass and
spin-decay length fixed at $\rho = \SI{1.20}{\kilo\gram\per\metre\cubed}$,
$m = \SI{0.43}{\kilo\gram}$ (the middle of the legal range
\SIrange{0.41}{0.45}{\kilo\gram}, \citealp{ifab2025}) and
$L = \SI{230}{\metre}$ (the middle of the measured range).
Section~\ref{sec:montecarlo} lets the mass and decay length vary and fixes the
density at the standard-atmosphere value of \SI{1.225}{\kilo\gram\per\metre\cubed};
a change in density of this size moves the predicted crossing point by less than
\SI{2.5}{\centi\metre} (Section~\ref{sec:fixed-results}).

% =====================================================================
\section{Exact Fits in the Well-Constrained Case}
\label{sec:exact-fits}
% =====================================================================

With the air density, ball mass and spin-decay length fixed, a trajectory is
determined by six numbers: the three components of the launch velocity
$\bm{v}_0$ and the three components of the spin $\bm{\omega}_0$. The two
observations also supply six numbers. In this section we ask which launch
velocities and spins reproduce both observations to within their rounding,
and where those trajectories cross the goal plane. The method applies to any
pair of observations; we apply it to the scenario of
Table~\ref{tab:scenario}.

\subsection{Spin Components}
\label{sec:spin-frame}

We describe the spin relative to the direction of launch. Let
$\hat{\bm{f}} = \bm{v}_0/|\bm{v}_0|$ point along the launch velocity, let
$\hat{\bm{e}}_r = \hat{\bm{f}}\times\hat{\bm{z}}/|\hat{\bm{f}}\times\hat{\bm{z}}|$
point horizontally to the right of the flight, and let
$\hat{\bm{e}}_u = \hat{\bm{e}}_r\times\hat{\bm{f}}$ point up. We write
\begin{equation}
  \bm{\omega}_0 = \omega_b\,\hat{\bm{e}}_r + \omega_s\,\hat{\bm{e}}_u
                + \omega_f\,\hat{\bm{f}},
  \label{eq:spinframe}
\end{equation}
where $\omega_b$ is backspin (positive values lift the ball, negative values
are topspin), $\omega_s$ is sidespin (positive values curl the ball to the
kicker's left, $+y$) and $\omega_f$ is screw spin about the direction of
travel. We quote spin rates in revolutions per second
(\si{\rev\per\second}).

\subsection{Why Screw spin is Set to Zero}
\label{sec:screw}

With all six unknowns free, the six observations can be matched exactly. We
solved for them by least squares from several starting points. The only exact
solutions we found need a screw spin of nearly \SI{1000}{\rev\per\second},
more than a hundred times the spin of measured kicks
\citep{tsukada2011, whiteside2010}. They arise because screw spin
produces no lift by itself (Equation~\ref{eq:sperp}); it acts only through the
small angle that opens between the spin axis and the velocity as the path
bends, so an enormous screw spin is needed to have any effect. These solutions
are artifacts of the six-equation count, not kicks. Adding up to
\SI{30}{\rev\per\second} of screw spin to our best fit below changes its
misses by less than \SI{0.5}{\milli\metre}, so we set $\omega_f = 0$. That
leaves five unknowns, $\bm{v}_0$, $\omega_b$ and $\omega_s$, for six observed
numbers. The system is overdetermined, so we cannot expect an exact fit and
instead ask for a fit within the precision of the data.

\subsection{Existence and Uniqueness}
\label{sec:uniqueness}

The reduction from six to five unknowns raises fundamental questions regarding
the existence and uniqueness of trajectories matching the observations.

\paragraph{Initial value problems versus multipoint boundary constraints.}
Under classical ordinary differential equation (ODE) theory, our equations of
motion $\ddot{\bm{r}} = \bm{f}(\bm{r}, \dot{\bm{r}}, \bm{\omega})$
(Equation~\ref{eq:accel}) define an autonomous dynamical system in the
six-dimensional phase space $(\bm{r}, \bm{v})$ (or seven-dimensional when
augmented by the arc length $s$ of Equation~\ref{eq:spindecay}). As derived in
Appendix~\ref{app:lipschitz}, the vector field is Lipschitz continuous on the
compact, convex physical flight domain where velocity remains strictly bounded
away from zero ($|\bm{v}| \ge v_{\mathrm{low}} > 0$).
For an initial value problem (IVP) where the initial position $\bm{r}(0) = \bm{r}_0$
and launch velocity $\dot{\bm{r}}(0) = \bm{v}_0$ are specified alongside fixed
ball parameters and spin $\bm{\omega}$, the Picard--Lindel{\"o}f
(Cauchy--Lipschitz) theorem \citep[Thm.~I.8.1]{hairer1993}
guarantees that there exists a unique solution $\bm{r}(t)$ forward in time. A
vital consequence of this uniqueness is that trajectories
corresponding to distinct initial states in the full phase space
can never cross or bifurcate.

In our scenario, however, the launch velocity $\bm{v}_0$ is unknown. Instead of
an initial value problem, we are presented with a multipoint boundary value
problem (BVP): the center of the ball is observed at three discrete points
$\bm{r}(t_0) = \bm{r}_0$, $\bm{r}(t_1) = \tilde{\bm{r}}_1$, and
$\bm{r}(t_2) = \tilde{\bm{r}}_2$ at times $t_0 = 0$,
$t_1 = \SI{0.05}{\second}$, and $t_2 = \SI{0.10}{\second}$
(Table~\ref{tab:scenario}). For boundary value problems, the existence and
uniqueness guarantees of the Picard--Lindel{\"o}f theorem do not automatically
apply, and the determinacy of the system depends strictly on the balance between
constraints and free parameters.

\paragraph{Degree of determinacy: constrained versus overconstrained systems.}
Each three-dimensional observation provides three scalar coordinate
constraints, yielding $3 \times 3 = 9$ constraints in total. Fixing the
initial position $\bm{r}(0) = \bm{r}_0$ at $t_0 = 0$ satisfies three of these
conditions, leaving six scalar constraints to be satisfied at $t_1$ and $t_2$:
\begin{equation}
  \bm{r}(t_1;\, \bm{p}) = \tilde{\bm{r}}_1, \qquad
  \bm{r}(t_2;\, \bm{p}) = \tilde{\bm{r}}_2,
  \label{eq:bvp-constraints}
\end{equation}
where $\bm{p}$ denotes the vector of unknown initial parameters.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Unconstrained six-parameter case ($6 = 6$):} If both the launch
    velocity $\bm{v}_0 \in \mathbb{R}^3$ and the full spin vector
    $\bm{\omega}_0 \in \mathbb{R}^3$ are treated as free parameters, the system
    possesses six unknown scalar parameters to satisfy the six scalar equations
    of Equation~\eqref{eq:bvp-constraints}. By the inverse function theorem
    applied to smooth dynamical flows \citep[Sec.~I.14]{hairer1993}, the system
    is formally determined, and exact mathematical roots exist.
    However, as shown in Section~\ref{sec:screw}, because screw spin $\omega_f$
    is parallel to the velocity vector and generates no transverse Magnus force
    to first order, the only exact roots require an unphysical spin rate of
    nearly \SI{1000}{\rev\per\second}.
  \item \textbf{Physical five-parameter case ($6 > 5$):} Setting screw spin to
    zero ($\omega_f = 0$) leaves five physical degrees of freedom: the three
    components of launch velocity $\bm{v}_0$ and the two transverse spin rates
    $(\omega_b, \omega_s)$. Matching the observations now requires satisfying
    six scalar coordinate equations with only five unknowns. The system is
    strictly \emph{overdetermined} (overconstrained).
\end{itemize}
Because $6 > 5$, an exact mathematical trajectory passing through all three
points simultaneously does not exist in general for an overdetermined system.
Even if the data were generated by an actual physical kick, real-world
measurements inevitably contain noise and discretization error, rendering exact
algebraic matching ill-posed and making parameter estimation for nonlinear ODEs
from discrete observations fundamentally an optimization problem \citep{ramsay2007}.

\paragraph{Finding the possible exact and consistent solutions.}
Because an exact algebraic zero residual ($\bm{r}(t_i) = \tilde{\bm{r}}_i$) is
exceedingly unlikely in practice, it is fitting to reframe the search for
solutions around physical consistency. In practice, the observed coordinates are rounded to the
nearest centimeter, meaning each true coordinate lies within a rounding box of
$\pm\SI{5}{\milli\metre}$. Any trajectory satisfying this Chebyshev ($\ell_\infty$)
error bound (defined formally in Section~\ref{sec:consistency}) represents an
indistinguishable, physically valid solution.

To isolate every consistent solution across the parameter space, we exploit a
natural kinematic decoupling. Over the brief observation window
($\Delta t \le \SI{0.10}{\second}$), the flight is overwhelmingly dominated by
initial velocity, while aerodynamic drag and Magnus forces act as small
perturbations (inducing only about \SI{1}{\centi\metre} of trajectory
deflection). Because the flow map is nearly ballistic over this short duration,
the mapping from launch velocity $\bm{v}_0$ to positions is strictly monotonic
and locally convex \citeneeded{monotonicity and local convexity of short-time ballistic parameter maps}.
Consequently, for any candidate transverse spin $(\omega_b, \omega_s)$, there
exists a unique, well-conditioned optimal launch velocity
$\bm{v}_0^*(\omega_b, \omega_s)$ that minimizes the coordinate residuals.

We find this optimal velocity using the Levenberg--Marquardt algorithm
\citep{levenberg1944, marquardt1963}, followed by a minimax linear program to
minimize the largest coordinate miss $\delta_{\max}$. By evaluating this
optimization across a dense, bounded grid of physically plausible spin rates
$(\omega_b, \omega_s) \in [-40, 40]^2\,\si{\rev\per\second}$, we systematically
sweep the entire parameter space to identify all consistent trajectories. From a
theoretical standpoint, if the observations were exact, noiseless measurements
from a real kick, the real-analyticity of the equations of motion on the
unsaturated flight domain (Appendix~\ref{app:lipschitz})
\citeneeded{real-analyticity of ODE flows and isolated roots theorem} would
guarantee that the exact solution set consists of isolated points, isolating a
single physical solution in the subsonic domain. Under finite measurement
rounding ($\pm\SI{5}{\milli\metre}$), this isolated point expands into the
compact, connected region of consistent trajectories mapped in
Section~\ref{sec:search}.

\subsection{Consistency with the data}
\label{sec:consistency}

The observed positions are given to the nearest centimeter, so each true
coordinate lies within \SI{5}{\milli\metre} of the stated value. Let
$\bm{r}(t;\, \bm{v}_0, \omega_b, \omega_s)$ be the model's position and
$\tilde{\bm{r}}_1$, $\tilde{\bm{r}}_2$ the observed positions at $t_1$ and
$t_2$. We call a trajectory \emph{consistent} with the data if all six
coordinate misses are at most \SI{5}{\milli\metre}:
\begin{equation}
  \delta_{\max} \;=\; \max_{i = 1,2}\;
  \big\| \bm{r}(t_i;\, \bm{v}_0, \omega_b, \omega_s) - \tilde{\bm{r}}_i \big\|_\infty
  \;\le\; \SI{5}{\milli\metre},
  \label{eq:consistent}
\end{equation}
where $\|\cdot\|_\infty$ is the largest of the three coordinate differences.
This is a hard bound rather than a statistical one, because rounding error
can never exceed half the last digit.

\subsection{Finding every consistent trajectory}
\label{sec:search}

The data fix the launch velocity sharply but the spin only weakly, so we
treat the two differently: we search over spin, and for each spin we solve
for the launch velocity.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Starting guess.} The constant-acceleration curve through
    $\bm{r}_0$, $\tilde{\bm{r}}_1$ and $\tilde{\bm{r}}_2$ has initial velocity
    \begin{equation}
      \bm{v}_{\mathrm{init}} = \frac{4\tilde{\bm{r}}_1 - \tilde{\bm{r}}_2 - 3\bm{r}_0}{2\,\Delta t},
      \qquad \Delta t = \SI{0.05}{\second},
      \label{eq:vinit}
    \end{equation}
    which for the scenario is $(30.4, 9.4, 6.9)$\,\si{\metre\per\second}.
  \item \textbf{Fit the launch velocity.} For a given spin
    $(\omega_b, \omega_s)$, we find the $\bm{v}_0$ that minimizes the sum of
    the six squared misses, using the Levenberg--Marquardt algorithm
    \citep{levenberg1944, marquardt1963, more1978} from
    $\bm{v}_{\mathrm{init}}$. Each evaluation integrates the equations of
    motion to $t_2$. The derivatives of the positions with respect to
    $\bm{v}_0$ are computed by central differences, integrating the
    perturbed trajectories together with the main one so that all of them
    use the same time steps.
  \item \textbf{Minimize the worst miss.} The least-squares fit makes the
    misses small on average, but Equation~\eqref{eq:consistent} limits the
    largest one. Near the least-squares solution the misses depend almost
    linearly on $\bm{v}_0$, so we find the change in $\bm{v}_0$ that
    minimizes the largest miss by solving a small linear program
    \citep{huangfu2018}. We then integrate the corrected trajectory again and
    keep the correction only if it reduces $\delta_{\max}$. This lowers the
    largest miss by about \SI{2}{\milli\metre} on average.
  \item \textbf{Scan the spin.} We repeat steps 2--3 on a grid of spins,
    $\omega_b$ and $\omega_s$ each from \SIrange{-40}{40}{\rev\per\second} in
    steps of \SI{0.5}{\rev\per\second}, a total of 25{,}921 spins. The
    consistent spins lie well inside the grid, so the grid does not cut off
    any solutions.
  \item \textbf{Fly each trajectory to the goal.} For every grid point we
    integrate the trajectory to $x = 11$ and record the crossing point.
\end{enumerate}
As an independent check on step 4, we also minimized the sum of squared
misses over all five unknowns at once, from 200 random starting spins of up to
\SI{30}{\rev\per\second}. Of the 200 starts, 162 converged to the same
minimum, next to the best grid point. The other 38 stopped on a ring of
spins near \SI{30}{\rev\per\second}. There $S_\perp \approx 0.64$, the drag in
Equation~\eqref{eq:cd} approaches its low-speed value and the lift of
Equation~\eqref{eq:cl} almost vanishes, so the direction of the spin no longer
matters. This ring is an edge of the lift model rather than a separate
solution, and none of its points is consistent with the data.

\subsection{What the fixed-parameter case shows}
\label{sec:fixed-lessons}

This analysis gives the crossing point implied by the model if the
observations are exact up to rounding. It also exposes three limits that
motivate the Monte Carlo method of the next section.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{The data determine the spin only weakly.} The extra curvature
    that spin must explain is about \SI{1}{\centi\metre}, the size of the
    rounding itself. When we moved the observations randomly within their
    rounding and refitted 40 times, the preferred spin ranged from
    \SIrange{16}{26}{\rev\per\second}. The spin estimate is therefore driven
    as much by how the numbers were rounded as by the physics.
  \item \textbf{The data favor spins at the top of the measured range.}
    Measured instep and curled kicks average \SIrange{3}{7.5}{\rev\per\second},
    and the fastest reach about \SI{9.3}{\rev\per\second}
    \citep{tsukada2011, whiteside2010}. Only spins at that upper edge fit the
    data, and only just (\SI{4.9}{\milli\metre} against the
    \SI{5}{\milli\metre} bound). The best fit spins at
    \SI{22.7}{\rev\per\second}, more than twice as fast as any measured kick.
    Either this kick spun unusually fast, or the rounding happens to
    exaggerate the curvature, as the previous point suggests.
  \item \textbf{The fits rely on the lift model beyond its data.} The
    consistent spins correspond to $S_\perp$ between $0.20$ and $0.60$, and
    \SI{93}{\percent} of them lie above $S_\perp = 0.31$, the largest spin
    parameter in the wind-tunnel data behind Equation~\eqref{eq:cl}. The exact
    location of the consistent region therefore depends on an extrapolated
    part of the lift curve.
\end{itemize}
The Monte Carlo method addresses these limits. It draws the spin only from
the range of measured kicks and lets the observations decide which spins in
that range are most likely; this also keeps $S_\perp$ below about 0.23
throughout the flight, inside the range of the lift data. It replaces the hard \SI{5}{\milli\metre} bound with
a measurement error of the same spread, so each draw is graded by how well it
fits rather than accepted or rejected. And it samples the ball's mass, spin
decay and drag and lift coefficients, so that the stated uncertainty includes
everything the observations cannot determine.

% =====================================================================
\section{Monte Carlo Model}
\label{sec:montecarlo}
% =====================================================================

Section~\ref{sec:exact-fits} showed that the two observations fix the launch
velocity to within about \SI{0.5}{\metre\per\second} but leave the spin
poorly determined, and that the answer depends on quantities we held fixed,
such as the ball's mass and its aerodynamic coefficients. The Monte Carlo
method treats every quantity the observations cannot determine as a random
variable with a distribution taken from measurements. Each sample (a
\emph{draw}) picks values for these quantities, finds the launch velocity
that best explains the observations, and flies the ball to the goal. Draws
whose physics explains the observations well count for more. The weighted
collection of crossing points is our prediction.

\subsection{Sampled quantities}
\label{sec:priors}

We write $\bm{\theta}$ for the quantities sampled in each draw. They and
their distributions are listed in Table~\ref{tab:priors}. All are drawn
independently and uniformly between bounds taken from the literature. We use
uniform distributions because the sources report a handful of kicks or balls
rather than a distribution, and because a uniform range makes the assumption
easy to state and to test.

\begin{table}[H]
  \centering
  \caption{Quantities sampled in each draw. All are independent and uniform.}
  \label{tab:priors}
  \small
  \begin{tabular}{llll}
    \toprule
    Quantity & Symbol & Range & Source \\
    \midrule
    Spin rate             & $|\bm{\omega}_0|$ & \SIrange{0}{9.3}{\rev\per\second} & \citet{tsukada2011} \\
    Spin-axis angle       & $\psi$            & \SIrange{-110}{110}{\degree} & \citet{tsukada2011, whiteside2010} \\
    Spin-decay length     & $L$               & \SIrange{110}{350}{\metre} & \citet{tsukada2008} \\
    Ball mass             & $m$               & \SIrange{0.41}{0.45}{\kilo\gram} & \citet{ifab2025} \\
    Drag factor           & $f_D$             & \numrange{1.00}{1.35} & \citet{kiratidis2018} \\
    Lift factor           & $f_L$             & \numrange{0.75}{1.00} & \citet{kiratidis2018} \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
\end{table}

\paragraph{Spin.} We use the launch-frame components of
Section~\ref{sec:spin-frame} and set the screw spin to zero, as in
Section~\ref{sec:screw}. The spin is then
\begin{equation}
  \omega_b = |\bm{\omega}_0| \cos\psi, \qquad
  \omega_s = |\bm{\omega}_0| \sin\psi, \qquad
  \omega_f = 0,
  \label{eq:spinprior}
\end{equation}
so $\psi = 0$ is pure backspin and $\psi = \pm\SI{90}{\degree}$ is pure
sidespin, with positive values curling the ball to the kicker's left.

The ranges come from two high-speed video studies of kicked balls.
\citet{tsukada2011} measured instep kicks spinning at $3.2 \pm 0.7$ and
$3.3 \pm 1.0$\,\si{\rev\per\second} (mean $\pm$ standard deviation of ten
kicks by each of two players), with the axis within about \SI{25}{\degree} of pure backspin,
and in-front (curled) kicks at $7.5 \pm 0.9$ and
$7.2 \pm 1.1$\,\si{\rev\per\second}, with the axis
\SIrange{98}{108}{\degree} from backspin. \citet{whiteside2010} found
$3.6 \pm 0.7$\,\si{\rev\per\second} for straight kicks and
$5.8 \pm 0.8$\,\si{\rev\per\second} for curved ones. The upper limit on the
rate is the mean plus two standard deviations of the fastest-spinning kicks,
$7.5 + 2 \times 0.9 = \SI{9.3}{\rev\per\second}$, which covers about
\SI{98}{\percent} of them if their rates are normally distributed. The angle range covers both kick types, curled in
either direction because we do not know which foot the kicker uses, and stops
at \SI{110}{\degree} because neither study found strong topspin. We do not
choose a kick type: the uniform rate and angle cover both without favoring
either.

\paragraph{Spin decay and mass.} The decay length $L$ of
Equation~\eqref{eq:spindecay} spans the range measured by
\citet{tsukada2008} (Section~\ref{sec:spin-decay}), and the mass spans the
legal range \citep{ifab2025}.

\paragraph{Drag and lift factors.} Each draw multiplies the drag and lift
coefficients of Equations~\eqref{eq:cd} and~\eqref{eq:cl} by independent
factors,
\begin{equation}
  C_D \;\to\; f_D\, C_D(v, S_\perp), \qquad
  C_L \;\to\; f_L\, C_L(v, S_\perp).
  \label{eq:factors}
\end{equation}
\citet{kiratidis2018} give no uncertainties for their curves, and we do not
know which ball is used. We therefore bound each factor by the spread of
their own model across the four World Cup balls they characterize. For each
ball we evaluate their drag curve (their Table~1 and Equations~5--7) and
their lift model (their Equation~9) over the speeds and spin parameters of

In their model a ball with more drag also has less lift. Drawing the two
factors independently ignores this link and allows combinations no real ball
has, which can only widen the predicted spread. The factor $f_D$ multiplies
the drag force only; the drag coefficient inside the lift model
(Equation~\eqref{eq:cl}) keeps its published value.

\paragraph{Fixed quantities.} The air density is not sampled. A goalkeeper
can know the conditions on the day, so we fix it at the sea-level value of
the standard atmosphere, $\rho = \SI{1.225}{\kilo\gram\per\metre\cubed}$
\citep{ussa1976}. The ball radius, gravity and still air are as in
Section~\ref{sec:assumptions}.

\paragraph{Measurement error.} The observed coordinates are rounded to the
nearest centimeter. A rounding error spread uniformly over
$\pm\SI{5}{\milli\metre}$ has standard deviation
$\SI{1}{\centi\metre}/\sqrt{12} = \SI{2.9}{\milli\metre}$. We model each
observed coordinate as the true position plus an independent Gaussian error
with this standard deviation, $\sigma = \SI{2.9}{\milli\metre}$. Unlike the
hard bound of Section~\ref{sec:consistency}, a Gaussian error lets us grade
how well each draw fits rather than only accept or reject it. Section~\ref{sec:results}
repeats the analysis with larger $\sigma$, as for a real tracking system.

\subsection{One draw}
\label{sec:draw}

Each draw takes five steps.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Sample $\bm{\theta}$} from Table~\ref{tab:priors}.
  \item \textbf{Fit the launch velocity.} Find the $\bm{v}_0^*$ that
    minimizes
    \begin{equation}
      \chi^2(\bm{v}_0) = \sum_{i=1}^{2}
        \frac{\big\|\bm{r}(t_i;\, \bm{v}_0, \bm{\theta}) - \tilde{\bm{r}}_i\big\|^2}{\sigma^2},
      \label{eq:chi2}
    \end{equation}
    the sum of the six squared misses in units of $\sigma$, by the
    Levenberg--Marquardt method of Section~\ref{sec:search}. Call the minimum
    $\chi^2_{\min}$.
  \item \textbf{Weight the draw} by
    \begin{equation}
      w = e^{-\chi^2_{\min}/2},
      \label{eq:weight}
    \end{equation}
    the likelihood of the observations under this draw's physics.
  \item \textbf{Sample the launch velocity} from a normal distribution
    centered on $\bm{v}_0^*$ with covariance $(J^\mathsf{T}J)^{-1}$, where $J$
    is the $6 \times 3$ matrix of derivatives of the six misses (divided by
    $\sigma$) with respect to $\bm{v}_0$. This is the uncertainty that the
    measurement error alone leaves in the launch velocity, about
    \SI{0.03}{\metre\per\second} in each component.
  \item \textbf{Fly the ball} from $\bm{r}_0$ with this launch velocity until
    its center reaches $x = 11$, and record the crossing point $(y, z)$ and
    whether the shot is on target, wide or over.
\end{enumerate}
Every draw has its own random seed, so the results are reproducible and
independent of how the draws are split across processors. We use
$N = 100{,}000$ draws per run.

\paragraph{Why this gives the right distribution.} By Bayes' theorem, the
probability of $\bm{\theta}$ and $\bm{v}_0$ given the observations $d$ is
proportional to the probability of the observations given them, times the
prior probability of $\bm{\theta}$:
\begin{equation}
  p(\bm{\theta}, \bm{v}_0 \mid d) \;\propto\;
  e^{-\chi^2(\bm{v}_0;\, \bm{\theta})/2}\; p(\bm{\theta}).
  \label{eq:bayes}
\end{equation}
We take all launch velocities to be equally likely beforehand, since the
observations fix $\bm{v}_0$ far more tightly than any prior knowledge could.
Step~1 samples $\bm{\theta}$ from $p(\bm{\theta})$, and the weight of step~3
supplies the remaining factor. This is importance sampling with the prior as
the proposal \citep{robert2004}. Near its minimum $\chi^2$ is close to
quadratic in $\bm{v}_0$, so for a given $\bm{\theta}$ the launch velocity is
approximately normal with the mean and covariance of step~4. Integrating that
normal over $\bm{v}_0$ would add a factor $|J^\mathsf{T}J|^{-1/2}$ to the
weight \citep{tierney1986}. We omit it: it varies by at most about
\SI{5}{\percent} across draws, because the launch velocity is set mainly by
the positions and times rather than by the physics.

The spread of the weights measures how much the observations narrow down
$\bm{\theta}$. We report the effective sample size
\begin{equation}
  N_{\mathrm{eff}} = \frac{\left(\sum_n w_n\right)^2}{\sum_n w_n^2},
  \label{eq:ess}
\end{equation}
which is $N$ when every draw fits equally well and approaches 1 when a single
draw dominates \citep{kish1965}.

\subsection{From draws to a prediction}
\label{sec:summaries}

The weighted crossing points sample the probability distribution of where
the ball crosses the goal plane. We estimate its density with a weighted
Gaussian kernel density estimate, with the bandwidth set by Scott's rule
using $N_{\mathrm{eff}}$ in place of $N$ \citep{scott1992}.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Most likely crossing point.} The peak of the density, found on
    a $120 \times 120$ grid and refined with the Nelder--Mead method
    \citep{nelder1965}.
  \item \textbf{Uncertainty regions.} The 68\% and 95\% highest-density
    regions: the smallest regions that contain that fraction of the
    probability. Each is the set of points where the density exceeds a
    threshold, chosen so that the draws above it carry 68\% or 95\% of the
    total weight \citep{hyndman1996}.
  \item \textbf{Spread.} The weighted mean and standard deviation of $y$ and
    $z$.
  \item \textbf{Outcome probabilities.} The weighted fractions of draws that
    are on target, wide or over.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Model variants}
\label{sec:variants}

To see how much the answer depends on the physics, we run three versions of
the model on the same random draws, so that differences between them are not
Monte Carlo noise.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Basic:} gravity, drag and lift
    (Equations~\eqref{eq:accel} and~\eqref{eq:factors}) with constant spin.
  \item \textbf{Standard:} the basic model with spin decay
    (Equation~\eqref{eq:spindecay}), buoyancy and added mass.
  \item \textbf{Extended:} the standard model with a knuckling force.
\end{itemize}
Wind is left out of all three (Section~\ref{sec:assumptions}).

\paragraph{Buoyancy and added mass.} The ball displaces a mass of air
$\rho V = \SI{6.8}{\gram}$, where $V = \tfrac{4}{3}\pi r^3$, about
\SI{1.6}{\percent} of its own mass. Buoyancy reduces its weight by $\rho V g$.
Accelerating the ball also accelerates the air around it, which adds half the
displaced mass to its inertia but not to its weight \citep{batchelor1967}.
With both effects, Equation~\eqref{eq:eom} becomes
\begin{equation}
  \left(m + \tfrac{1}{2}\rho V\right) \dot{\bm{v}}
  = (m - \rho V)\,\bm{g} + \bm{F}_D + \bm{F}_L .
  \label{eq:eom-air}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Knuckling.} A ball kicked with little spin can swerve erratically
as its wake sheds unevenly \citep{mizota2013}. We model this as an
oscillating sideways force,
\begin{equation}
  \bm{F}_k = \tfrac{1}{2}\rho A\, C_k\, |\bm{v}|^2\,
  \min\!\left(1, \frac{\omega_c}{|\bm{\omega}|}\right)
  \sin(2\pi f t + \phi)\, \hat{\bm{k}},
  \label{eq:knuckle}
\end{equation}
where $\hat{\bm{k}}$ is a unit vector perpendicular to the launch velocity.
Its direction around the flight path and the phase $\phi$ are drawn uniformly
in each draw. We take the peak coefficient $C_k = 0.10$, the low end of the
forces \citet{hong2010} measured on kicked knuckleballs (about \SI{2}{\newton}
at \SIrange{20}{30}{\metre\per\second}), and their average frequency
$f = \SI{3.5}{\hertz}$. The force is at full strength up to
$\omega_c = \SI{1}{\rev\per\second}$, below which \citet{mizota2013} observed
knuckling, and weakens in proportion to $1/|\bm{\omega}|$ above it, because a
spinning ball turns the force around and averages it out. Where the two
sources differ we take the more modest value, so the extended model is more
likely to understate knuckling than to overstate it.

\appendix
\section{Lipschitz Continuity of the Equations of Motion}
\label{app:lipschitz}
% =====================================================================

In this appendix, we establish that our full suite of equations of motion across
Sections~\ref{sec:simple-model} and~\ref{sec:montecarlo}
(Equations~\ref{eq:accel}, \ref{eq:spindecay}, \ref{eq:eom-air},
and~\ref{eq:knuckle}) is Lipschitz continuous on the physical flight domain of
a penalty kick, thereby justifying the application of the Picard--Lindel{\"o}f
existence and uniqueness theorem in Section~\ref{sec:uniqueness}.

\subsection{First-order state-space formulation and convex flight domain}

Let $\bm{y} = (\bm{r}, \bm{v}, s)^\top \in \mathbb{R}^7$ denote the combined
state vector comprising the ball's center position $\bm{r} \in \mathbb{R}^3$,
velocity $\bm{v} \in \mathbb{R}^3$ (with speed $v = |\bm{v}|$), and arc length
travelled $s \in \mathbb{R}$. Equations~\eqref{eq:accel}
and~\eqref{eq:spindecay} form the autonomous first-order system
\begin{equation}
  \dot{\bm{y}} = \bm{F}(\bm{y}) =
  \begin{bmatrix}
    \bm{v} \\
    \bm{g} + \bm{a}_D(\bm{v}, s) + \bm{a}_L(\bm{v}, s) \\
    |\bm{v}|
  \end{bmatrix},
  \label{eq:app-first-order}
\end{equation}
where the drag and Magnus lift accelerations are
\begin{equation}
  \bm{a}_D(\bm{v}, s) = -k\, C_D(v, S_\perp)\, v\,\bm{v}, \qquad
  \bm{a}_L(\bm{v}, s) = k\, C_L(v, S_\perp)\, v^2\, \hat{\bm{n}},
  \label{eq:app-aero-accel}
\end{equation}
with $\bm{\omega}(s) = \bm{\omega}_0\, e^{-s/L}$,
$\hat{\bm{n}} = (\bm{\omega}(s)\times\bm{v})/|\bm{\omega}(s)\times\bm{v}|$, and
$S_\perp = r|\bm{\omega}(s)\times\bm{v}|/v^2$.

Although the Euclidean norm $|\bm{v}|$ is not differentiable at $\bm{v} = \bm{0}$
and $S_\perp$ is singular as $v \to 0$, a penalty kick remains in high-speed
forward flight throughout its journey to the goal plane ($x = \SI{11}{\metre}$).
Let $\hat{\bm{f}} = \bm{v}_0 / |\bm{v}_0|$ denote the unit launch direction. We
define the physical state domain
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{D} = \mathcal{B}_r \times \mathcal{C}_v \times [0, s_{\max}]
  \subset \mathbb{R}^7,
  \label{eq:app-domain}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{B}_r = \{\bm{r} \in \mathbb{R}^3 : |\bm{r}| \le R_{\max}\}$,
$s_{\max} > 0$, and
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{C}_v = \big\{ \bm{v} \in \mathbb{R}^3 \;\big|\;
  \bm{v}\cdot\hat{\bm{f}} \ge v_{\mathrm{low}}, \;\;
  |\bm{v}| \le v_{\mathrm{high}} \big\},
  \qquad 0 < v_{\mathrm{low}} < v_{\mathrm{high}} < \infty
  \label{eq:app-vdomain}
\end{equation}
(for penalty kicks, $v_{\mathrm{low}} = \SI{10}{\metre\per\second}$,
$v_{\mathrm{high}} = \SI{45}{\metre\per\second}$, and
$R_{\max} = s_{\max} = \SI{20}{\metre}$). Because $\mathcal{C}_v$ is the
intersection of the closed half-space
$\{\bm{v}\cdot\hat{\bm{f}} \ge v_{\mathrm{low}}\}$ and the closed Euclidean ball
$\{|\bm{v}| \le v_{\mathrm{high}}\}$, $\mathcal{C}_v$ and $\mathcal{D}$ are
strictly \emph{compact and convex}, and every $\bm{v} \in \mathcal{C}_v$
satisfies
\begin{equation}
  0 < v_{\mathrm{low}} \le \bm{v}\cdot\hat{\bm{f}} \le |\bm{v}| \le v_{\mathrm{high}}.
  \label{eq:app-vbounds}
\end{equation}
On a compact, convex domain $\mathcal{D}$, any continuously differentiable
($C^1$) mapping $\bm{h}: \mathcal{D} \to \mathbb{R}^m$ satisfies the Mean Value
Inequality
$\|\bm{h}(\bm{y}_1) - \bm{h}(\bm{y}_2)\|_2 \le (\sup_{\bm{y}\in\mathcal{D}}\|D\bm{h}(\bm{y})\|_2)\|\bm{y}_1 - \bm{y}_2\|_2$,
and is therefore Lipschitz continuous on $\mathcal{D}$.

\subsection{Non-vanishing transverse spin identity}

With screw spin set to zero ($\omega_f = 0$, Equation~\ref{eq:spinframe}), the
initial spin vector $\bm{\omega}_0 = \omega_b\,\hat{\bm{e}}_r + \omega_s\,\hat{\bm{e}}_u$
is orthogonal to $\hat{\bm{f}}$. Decomposing any velocity $\bm{v} \in \mathcal{C}_v$
into its components parallel and perpendicular to $\hat{\bm{f}}$,
$\bm{v} = (\bm{v}\cdot\hat{\bm{f}})\hat{\bm{f}} + \bm{v}_\perp$ with
$\bm{v}_\perp \perp \hat{\bm{f}}$, the cross product
$\bm{w}(\bm{v}, s) \equiv \bm{\omega}(s)\times\bm{v}$ splits into two mutually
orthogonal terms: $(\bm{v}\cdot\hat{\bm{f}})(\bm{\omega}_0\times\hat{\bm{f}}) \perp \hat{\bm{f}}$
and $(\bm{\omega}_0\times\bm{v}_\perp) \parallel \hat{\bm{f}}$. By the
Pythagorean theorem and Equation~\eqref{eq:app-vbounds},
\begin{equation}
  |\bm{w}(\bm{v}, s)|
  = e^{-s/L} \sqrt{(\bm{v}\cdot\hat{\bm{f}})^2 |\bm{\omega}_0|^2
                   + |\bm{\omega}_0\times\bm{v}_\perp|^2}
  \;\ge\; |\bm{\omega}_0|\, e^{-s_{\max}/L}\, v_{\mathrm{low}}
  \;\equiv\; w_{\min}.
  \label{eq:app-wmin}
\end{equation}
Consequently, for any kick with non-zero spin ($\bm{\omega}_0 \neq \bm{0}$),
$|\bm{w}(\bm{v}, s)| \ge w_{\min} > 0$ and
$S_\perp \ge r w_{\min}/v_{\mathrm{high}}^2 > 0$ strictly throughout
$\mathcal{D}$, preventing any vanishing-denominator singularity in
$\hat{\bm{n}} = \bm{w}/|\bm{w}|$. Conversely, if $\bm{\omega}_0 = \bm{0}$, then
$\bm{w} \equiv \bm{0}$, $S_\perp \equiv 0$, and $\bm{a}_L \equiv \bm{0}$
identically across $\mathcal{D}$.

\subsection{Component-by-component Lipschitz verification}

\paragraph{Kinematics, gravity, and spin decay.}
The position derivative $\bm{F}_r(\bm{y}) = \bm{v}$ is linear with Jacobian
$\bm{I}_3$, and is thus globally Lipschitz with constant $L_r = 1$. The
arc-length rate $F_s(\bm{y}) = |\bm{v}|$ satisfies
$\big||\bm{v}_1| - |\bm{v}_2|\big| \le |\bm{v}_1 - \bm{v}_2|$ by the reverse
triangle inequality ($L_s = 1$), and is $C^\infty$ on $\mathcal{C}_v$ since
$v \ge v_{\mathrm{low}} > 0$, with $\nabla_{\bm{v}}|\bm{v}| = \bm{v}/v$. Gravity
$\bm{g}$ is constant ($L_g = 0$), and the spin decay factor $e^{-s/L}$ is
$C^\infty$ on $[0, s_{\max}]$ with derivative bounded in magnitude by $1/L$.

\paragraph{Aerodynamic drag $\bm{a}_D(\bm{v}, s)$.}
Substituting Equation~\eqref{eq:cd} into $\bm{a}_D$ gives
\begin{equation}
  \bm{a}_D(\bm{v}, s)
  = -k\, C_{D0}(v)\, v\,\bm{v}
    - 0.514\, k\, r\, |\bm{w}(\bm{v}, s)|\, \frac{\bm{v}}{v}.
  \label{eq:app-drag-expanded}
\end{equation}
In Equation~\eqref{eq:cd0}, $C_{D0}(v)$ is a linear combination of logistic
sigmoids $(1 + e^{\pm(v - v_*)/v_s})^{-1}$ whose denominators are strictly
positive for all $v \in \mathbb{R}$; hence $C_{D0}(v)$ is real-analytic
($C^\omega$) on $[v_{\mathrm{low}}, v_{\mathrm{high}}]$ with bounded derivative.
On $\mathcal{C}_v$, both $v\bm{v}$ and $\bm{v}/v$ are $C^\infty$, with spectral
norms of their Jacobians bounded by
\begin{equation}
  \left\|\frac{\partial(v\bm{v})}{\partial\bm{v}}\right\|_2
  = \left\|v\bm{I}_3 + \frac{\bm{v}\bm{v}^\top}{v}\right\|_2
  = 2v \le 2v_{\mathrm{high}}, \qquad
  \left\|\frac{\partial(\bm{v}/v)}{\partial\bm{v}}\right\|_2
  = \left\|\frac{1}{v}\left(\bm{I}_3 - \frac{\bm{v}\bm{v}^\top}{v^2}\right)\right\|_2
  = \frac{1}{v} \le \frac{1}{v_{\mathrm{low}}}.
\end{equation}
Furthermore, $(\bm{v}, s) \mapsto |\bm{w}(\bm{v}, s)|$ is $C^\infty$ on
$\mathcal{D}$ whenever $\bm{\omega}_0 \neq \bm{0}$ (by
Equation~\ref{eq:app-wmin}) and identically zero when $\bm{\omega}_0 = \bm{0}$.
Because $\bm{a}_D \in C^\infty(\mathcal{D})$ on the compact, convex set
$\mathcal{D}$, $\bm{a}_D$ is Lipschitz continuous on $\mathcal{D}$ with finite
constant $L_D = \sup_{\mathcal{D}}\|D\bm{a}_D\|_2 < \infty$.

\paragraph{Magnus lift $\bm{a}_L(\bm{v}, s)$.}
When $\bm{\omega}_0 = \bm{0}$, $\bm{a}_L(\bm{v}, s) \equiv \bm{0}$, which is
trivially Lipschitz ($L_L = 0$). When $\bm{\omega}_0 \neq \bm{0}$, substituting
$S_\perp = r|\bm{w}|/v^2$ and Equation~\eqref{eq:cl} into
Equation~\eqref{eq:lift} yields
\begin{equation}
  \bm{a}_L(\bm{v}, s)
  = \frac{1.15\, k\, r^{0.83}}{C_{D0}(0) - C_{D,\mathrm{ref}}}\;
    v^{0.34}\; \bm{\psi}\big(\bm{w}(\bm{v}, s)\big)\; \Phi(\bm{v}, s),
  \label{eq:app-lift-expanded}
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}
  \bm{\psi}(\bm{w}) = |\bm{w}|^{-0.17}\bm{w}, \qquad
  \Phi(\bm{v}, s) = C_{D0}(0) - \min\!\big(C_D(v, S_\perp),\, C_{D0}(0)\big).
\end{equation}
We examine each factor of Equation~\eqref{eq:app-lift-expanded} on $\mathcal{D}$:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item The speed factor $v^{0.34}$ is $C^\infty$ on $\mathcal{C}_v$, bounded by
    $v_{\mathrm{high}}^{0.34}$, with gradient norm
    $|\nabla_{\bm{v}}(v^{0.34})| = 0.34\, v^{-0.66} \le 0.34\, v_{\mathrm{low}}^{-0.66}$.
  \item By Equation~\eqref{eq:app-wmin}, $|\bm{w}(\bm{v}, s)| \ge w_{\min} > 0$
    throughout $\mathcal{D}$, so $\bm{\psi}(\bm{w})$ is $C^\infty$ on
    $\mathcal{D}$ with bounded Jacobian
    \begin{equation}
      \frac{\partial\bm{\psi}}{\partial\bm{w}}
      = |\bm{w}|^{-0.17}\left(\bm{I}_3 - 0.17\,\frac{\bm{w}\bm{w}^\top}{|\bm{w}|^2}\right),
      \qquad
      \left\|\frac{\partial\bm{\psi}}{\partial\bm{w}}\right\|_2
      = |\bm{w}|^{-0.17} \le w_{\min}^{-0.17} < \infty.
    \end{equation}
    Since $\bm{w}(\bm{v}, s) = \bm{\omega}(s)\times\bm{v}$ is $C^\infty$ with
    bounded derivatives on $\mathcal{D}$, the composite map
    $(\bm{v}, s) \mapsto \bm{\psi}(\bm{w}(\bm{v}, s))$ is $C^\infty$, bounded,
    and Lipschitz on $\mathcal{D}$.
  \item For the drag-attenuation factor $\Phi(\bm{v}, s)$, the scalar clamping
    map $u \mapsto \min(u, c)$ is globally $1$-Lipschitz on $\mathbb{R}$:
    $|\min(u_1, c) - \min(u_2, c)| \le |u_1 - u_2|$. Because
    $C_D(v, S_\perp)$ is $C^\infty$ and Lipschitz on $\mathcal{D}$ with constant
    $L_{C_D} = \sup_{\mathcal{D}}\|\nabla C_D\|_2 < \infty$, the composition
    $\Phi(\bm{v}, s)$ is bounded ($0 \le \Phi \le C_{D0}(0)$) and Lipschitz
    continuous across all of $\mathcal{D}$ with constant $L_\Phi \le L_{C_D}$,
    including across the piecewise-smooth saturation threshold
    $C_D(v, S_\perp) = C_{D0}(0)$ near $\SI{30}{\rev\per\second}$ discussed in
    Section~\ref{sec:search}. Moreover, on the unsaturated physical regime where
    $C_D(v, S_\perp) < C_{D0}(0)$, $\Phi(\bm{v}, s) = C_{D0}(0) - C_D(v, S_\perp)$
    is strictly real-analytic ($C^\omega$).
\end{enumerate}
Because the product of bounded Lipschitz functions on a compact domain is
Lipschitz continuous, $\bm{a}_L(\bm{v}, s)$ is Lipschitz continuous on
$\mathcal{D}$ with a finite constant $L_L < \infty$.

\paragraph{Standard and extended model variants (Section~\ref{sec:variants}).}
The remaining model variants in Section~\ref{sec:montecarlo} preserve Lipschitz
continuity with respect to the state $\bm{y}$ and continuity in time $t$:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Drag and lift scaling factors (Equation~\ref{eq:factors}):}
    Multiplying $C_D$ and $C_L$ by the fixed per-trajectory constants
    $f_D, f_L > 0$ simply rescales the Lipschitz constants $L_D$ and $L_L$ by
    $f_D$ and $f_L$.
  \item \textbf{Buoyancy and added mass (Equation~\ref{eq:eom-air}):} Dividing
    Equation~\eqref{eq:eom-air} by the effective mass
    $m_{\mathrm{eff}} = m + \tfrac{1}{2}\rho V > 0$ replaces $\bm{g}$ with the
    constant vector $\frac{m - \rho V}{m_{\mathrm{eff}}}\bm{g}$ ($L = 0$) and
    multiplies $\bm{a}_D$ and $\bm{a}_L$ by the constant factor
    $m / m_{\mathrm{eff}} < 1$.
  \item \textbf{Knuckling force (Equation~\ref{eq:knuckle}):} The knuckling
    acceleration is
    \begin{equation}
      \bm{a}_k(t, \bm{v}, s)
      = \frac{m}{m_{\mathrm{eff}}}\, k\, C_k\, v^2\,
        G(s)\, \sin(2\pi f t + \phi)\, \hat{\bm{k}},
      \qquad G(s) = \min\!\left(1,\, \frac{\omega_c}{|\bm{\omega}_0|}e^{s/L}\right).
    \end{equation}
    This term is $C^\infty$ in $t$ with $|\sin(2\pi f t + \phi)| \le 1$. On
    $\mathcal{C}_v$, $v^2 = \bm{v}\cdot\bm{v}$ is $C^\infty$ and bounded by
    $v_{\mathrm{high}}^2$ with $|\nabla_{\bm{v}}(v^2)| = 2v \le 2v_{\mathrm{high}}$.
    For the spin-weakening factor $G(s)$, whenever
    $(\omega_c / |\bm{\omega}_0|)e^{s/L} < 1$, its derivative with respect to
    $s$ satisfies
    $0 \le G'(s) = \frac{1}{L}\frac{\omega_c}{|\bm{\omega}_0|}e^{s/L} < \frac{1}{L}$,
    whereas on the saturated branch $(\omega_c / |\bm{\omega}_0|)e^{s/L} > 1$
    (including the limit $\bm{\omega}_0 \to \bm{0}$), $G(s) \equiv 1$ and
    $G'(s) = 0$. Thus $G(s)$ is bounded in $[0, 1]$ and globally
    $(1/L)$-Lipschitz in $s$ uniformly for all $\bm{\omega}_0$. Consequently,
    $\bm{a}_k(t, \bm{v}, s)$ is continuous in $t$ and Lipschitz continuous in
    $(\bm{v}, s)$ on $\mathcal{D}$ with a finite constant $L_k < \infty$.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Synthesis}

Combining the bounds for all components of $\bm{F}$ on $\mathcal{D}$, we obtain
for any $\bm{y}_1, \bm{y}_2 \in \mathcal{D}$ and $t \ge 0$:
\begin{equation}
  \|\bm{F}(t, \bm{y}_1) - \bm{F}(t, \bm{y}_2)\|_2
  \;\le\; L_{\mathrm{tot}}\, \|\bm{y}_1 - \bm{y}_2\|_2,
  \qquad L_{\mathrm{tot}} = \sqrt{2 + (L_D + L_L + L_k)^2} < \infty
  \label{eq:app-total-lipschitz}
\end{equation}
(with $L_k = 0$ in the basic and standard models).
Therefore, $\bm{F}$ is Lipschitz continuous on $\mathcal{D}$. By the
Picard--Lindel{\"o}f theorem \citep[Thm.~I.8.1]{hairer1993}, for any initial state
$\bm{y}(0) = (\bm{r}_0, \bm{v}_0, 0)^\top \in \mathcal{D}$, there exists a
unique trajectory $\bm{y}(t)$ in $\mathcal{D}$. Furthermore, on the unsaturated
physical regime ($C_D < C_{D0}(0)$ and $\bm{\omega}_0 \neq \bm{0}$), every
building block of $\bm{F}$ is real-analytic ($C^\omega$), which by the analytic
Cauchy--Kowalevski theorem guarantees that the forward flow map
$(\bm{v}_0, \omega_b, \omega_s) \mapsto \bm{r}(t)$ is real-analytic as well.
