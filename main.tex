\section{Introduction}
% =====================================================================

In a penalty kick, one player shoots at a goal defended only by the
goalkeeper. The ball starts on a mark \SI{11}{\metre} in front of a goal
\SI{7.32}{\metre} wide and \SI{2.44}{\metre} high \citep{ifab2025}.
A well-struck penalty covers that distance in about \SI{0.4}{\second}, too
fast for the goalkeeper to wait and see where the ball goes, as human visual
reaction time alone accounts for $\approx\SI{0.2}{\second}$ before physical
movement even begins \citep{teichner1954}. To block the
kick, the goalkeeper has to anticipate the trajectory from the first
instants of the ball's flight. This paper asks how well that can be done:
given the ball's position at two moments shortly after the kick, where will
it most likely cross the goal line, and how sure can we be about its final position?

The first section of the ball's trajectory, however, contains only a limited amount of information about the forces that
decide where the ball ends up. In the scenario specified in the problem statement, over the first \SI{0.1}{\second} the ball
travels about \SI{3}{\metre}, and the curve that spin puts into its path
is only about \SI{1}{\centi\metre}, the same size as the precision of the
positions we are given. By the time the ball reaches the goal, the same spin
has moved it by tens of centimeters. Drag, which slows the ball, depends on
its speed and spin in ways that cannot be measured from two positions.
Predicting the point at which the balls crosses the plane of the goal is therefore
a complex extrapolation problem with significant uncertainty which must be quantified.

Our approach combines a physical model of the ball's flight with a
statistical analysis of what the observations cannot determine.
Section~\ref{sec:restatement} restates the problem and our interpretation
of it. Section~\ref{sec:simple-model} introduces the forces on the ball and
the assumptions of a simplified model. Section~\ref{sec:exact-fits} applies
that model in the case where the problem is well constrained, and finds the
trajectories that pass through the given positions to within their rounding.
Section~\ref{sec:montecarlo} extends this to a Monte Carlo method that samples uncertain quantities from distributions and weights each sample according to how well it explains the observations.
Section~\ref{sec:results} gives the predicted crossing point and its
uncertainty for the given scenario, together with tests of the method on
simulated kicks, and Section~\ref{sec:discussion} discusses the strengths
and weaknesses of the approach.

\section{Problem Restatement}
\label{sec:restatement}
% =====================================================================

A penalty kick is taken from the penalty mark. We use the coordinate system
of the problem, in meters:
\begin{itemize}
  \item the origin is at the penalty mark;
  \item the $x$-axis points toward the goal, which lies in the plane $x = 11$;
  \item the $y$-axis runs across the goal, with $+y$ on the kicker's left;
  \item the $z$-axis points up.
\end{itemize}
The goal mouth is the region $-3.66 < y < 3.66$, $0 < z < 2.44$ of the
plane $x = 11$. At $t = 0$ the center of the ball is at
$\bm{r}_0 = (0, 0, 0.11)$, one ball radius above the mark. The position of
the ball is then observed at $t_1 = \SI{0.05}{\second}$ and
$t_2 = \SI{0.10}{\second}$.

The problem asks us to develop a general method that, given the coordinates
of the ball at $t_1$ and $t_2$,
\begin{enumerate}
  \item predicts the point at which the ball is most likely to enter the goal, and
  \item estimates the uncertainty of that prediction.
\end{enumerate}
We are to test the method and apply it to the scenario in
Table~\ref{tab:scenario}.

  \centering
  \caption{Observed positions of the ball's center in the given scenario.
    The position at $t = 0$ is given exactly.}
  \label{tab:scenario}

    \toprule
    {$t$ (\si{\second})} & {$x$ (\si{\metre})} & {$y$ (\si{\metre})} & {$z$ (\si{\metre})} \\

  \end{tabular}
\end{table}

We interpret a general method as being capable of
taking any two observed positions as input; the given scenario is one application of this method we test in depth. We define the point where the ball enters the goal to
be the $(y, z)$ position of its center when the center reaches the goal plane
$x = 11$; a shot whose center crosses outside the goal mouth is counted as
\emph{wide} ($|y| \geq 3.66$) or \emph{over} ($z \geq 2.44$). Given that he
observed positions are rounded to the nearest centimeter, we note that each coordinate is
known only to within $\pm\SI{5}{\milli\metre}$. Therefore, any trajectory that passes
within this distance of every observation is consistent with the data.

\section{Simplified Model and Assumptions}
\label{sec:simple-model}
% =====================================================================

In order to understand the flight characteristics of the ball, we first build a simplistic deterministic model. Given the ball's launch velocity, its
spin, and a few physical constants, we apply this method to predict the the entire path of a ball. The method in
Sections~\ref{sec:exact-fits} and~\ref{sec:montecarlo} then uses this model to work
backward from the two observed positions.

\subsection{Forces on the ball}
\label{sec:forces}

Once the ball has left the foot, three primary forces act on it
(Figure~\ref{fig:forces}): gravity, aerodynamic drag, and the Magnus lift that depends on spin. Writing $\bm{r}(t)$ for the position of the ball's center and
$\bm{v} = \dot{\bm{r}}$ for its velocity,
\begin{equation}
  m\,\dot{\bm{v}} \;=\; m\bm{g} \;+\; \bm{F}_D \;+\; \bm{F}_L ,
  \label{eq:eom}
\end{equation}
with $m$ the ball's mass and $\bm{g} = (0, 0, -g)$, $g = \SI{9.81}{\metre\per\second\squared}$.

Drag opposes the velocity and grows with the square of the
speed in the high-Reynolds-number regime characteristic of macroscopic balls in air \citep{timmerman1999}:
\begin{equation}
  \bm{F}_D = -\tfrac{1}{2}\rho A\, C_D\, |\bm{v}|\,\bm{v},
  \label{eq:drag}
\end{equation}
where $\rho$ is the air density, $A = \pi r^2$ is the ball's cross-section and
$C_D$ is the drag coefficient.

A spinning ball drags air around with it, deflecting
the flow and pushing the ball sideways to its direction of travel
\citep{kray2014}. The force
is perpendicular to both the velocity and the spin axis \citep{kiratidis2018}:
\begin{equation}
  \bm{F}_L = \tfrac{1}{2}\rho A\, C_L\, |\bm{v}|^2\,
             \frac{\bm{\omega} \times \bm{v}}{|\bm{\omega} \times \bm{v}|},
  \label{eq:lift}
\end{equation}
where $\bm{\omega}$ is the spin (angular velocity) and $C_L$ is the lift
coefficient. Backspin lifts the ball, topspin makes it dip and sidespin makes
it curl.

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.5\linewidth]{forces.png}
  \caption{Forces on the ball in flight: gravity $m\bm{g}$, drag $\bm{F}_D$
    opposite the velocity $\bm{v}$, and Magnus lift $\bm{F}_L$ perpendicular to
    both $\bm{v}$ and the spin $\bm{\omega}$. Here $\bm{\omega}$ points out of
    the page, which for a ball moving to the right is backspin, so the lift
    points up.}
  \label{fig:forces}
\end{figure}

Dividing Equation~\eqref{eq:eom} by $m$ gives the equations we integrate,
\begin{equation}
  \dot{\bm{r}} = \bm{v}, \qquad
  \dot{\bm{v}} = \bm{g} \;-\; k\, C_D\, |\bm{v}|\,\bm{v}
                 \;+\; k\, C_L\, |\bm{v}|^2\, \hat{\bm{n}},
  \qquad k = \frac{\rho A}{2m},
  \label{eq:accel}
\end{equation}
with $\hat{\bm{n}}$ the unit vector along $\bm{\omega}\times\bm{v}$. For a
regulation ball ($m = \SI{0.43}{\kilo\gram}$, $r = \SI{0.11}{\metre}$) in air
of density \SI{1.20}{\kilo\gram\per\metre\cubed}, $k = \SI{0.053}{\per\metre}$.
At \SI{32}{\metre\per\second}, the launch speed we fit in
Section~\ref{sec:exact-fits}, drag decelerates a
non-spinning ball by about \SI{8}{\metre\per\second\squared}, using the drag
coefficient $C_D \approx 0.15$ that \citet{kiratidis2018} fit to wind-tunnel
measurements of the Teamgeist ball \citep{asai2007}
(Section~\ref{sec:coefficients}). A spin of \SI{9.3}{\rev\per\second}, at the
top of the range measured when players' kicks are filmed with high-speed
cameras (\citealp{tsukada2011}; Section~\ref{sec:kick-spin}), gives a lift of about
\SI{16}{\metre\per\second\squared}.

\subsection{Drag and lift coefficients}
\label{sec:coefficients}

The aerodynamic coefficients $C_D$ and $C_L$ are not constants. They depend
dynamically on the ball's instantaneous speed and on the ratio of surface
rotational speed to translational velocity, characterized by the non-dimensional
spin parameter. Because only the component of angular velocity orthogonal to the
flight path deflects the oncoming airflow, we define the \emph{perpendicular}
spin parameter. \citet{kiratidis2018} define the spin parameter as
$S_p = r\omega/v$ (their Eq.~4) for a ball spinning about an axis perpendicular
to its flight (their Fig.~1), and give the lift force the direction
$\bm{\omega}\times\bm{v}$, whose magnitude $|\bm{\omega}||\bm{v}|\sin\theta$
counts only the perpendicular part of the spin. For a general spin axis we
therefore replace $\omega$ by its perpendicular component:
\begin{equation}
  S_\perp = \frac{r\,|\bm{\omega}\times\bm{v}|}{|\bm{v}|^2}
          = \frac{r\,|\bm{\omega}|\sin\theta}{|\bm{v}|},
  \label{eq:sperp}
\end{equation}
where $\theta$ represents the angle between the spin axis and the velocity. Spin
directed strictly along the trajectory (``rifle'' or screw spin, $\theta = 0$)
generates no cross-stream pressure imbalance and therefore no Magnus deflection.
Wind-tunnel measurements of the Magnus force on soccer balls likewise spin the
ball about an axis perpendicular to the flow and use the same spin parameter,
$S_P = \omega D/(2u_\infty)$ \citep{kray2014}. Because the soccer ball decelerates due
to drag and curves under lift, both $S_\perp$ and the resulting force
coefficients are evaluated dynamically at each numerical integration step.

We parameterize both coefficients using the empirical models of
\citet{kiratidis2018}, who fitted experimental datasets across modern FIFA match
balls, including the wind-tunnel measurements of \citet{asai2007} and the
high-speed trajectory tracking data of \citet{goff2010}. Specifically, we adopt
their parameters calibrated for the thermally bonded 14-panel Adidas Teamgeist.

For a non-spinning ball, the drag coefficient exhibits a classic boundary-layer
transition (\emph{the drag crisis}) near \SI{13}{\metre\per\second}, followed by
a flat turbulent plateau across game-speed regimes \citep[Eq.~5]{kiratidis2018}:
\begin{equation}
  C_{D0}(v) = \frac{a - b_{\min}}{1 + e^{(v - v_c)/v_s}} + b_{\min}
  + \frac{v - v_{\min}}{1 + e^{-(v - v_{\min})/v_s}}\,
    \frac{b_{\max} - b_{\min}}{v_{\max} - v_{\min}},
  \label{eq:cd0}
\end{equation}
with $v = |\bm{v}|$, subcritical drag $a = 0.4927$, critical speed
$v_c = \SI{12.58}{\metre\per\second}$, transition width
$v_s = \SI{1.071}{\metre\per\second}$, minimum post-crisis drag
$b_{\min} = 0.1440$, plateau slope bound $b_{\max} = 0.1540$, and speed interval
$[v_{\min}, v_{\max}] = [\SI{23.17}{}, \SI{35.00}{}]\,\si{\metre\per\second}$.

Boundary-layer rotation introduces an additional transverse drag penalty that
scales linearly with $S_\perp$ \citep[Eq.~7]{kiratidis2018}:
\begin{equation}
  C_D(v, S_\perp) = C_{D0}(v) + 0.514\, S_\perp .
  \label{eq:cd}
\end{equation}

The lift coefficient follows a sub-linear power law in $S_\perp$, attenuated if
drag approaches subcritical baseline levels \citep[Eqs.~8--9]{kiratidis2018}:
\begin{equation}
  C_L(v, S_\perp) = 1.15\, S_\perp^{0.83}\;
  \frac{C_{D0}(0) - \min\!\big(C_D(v, S_\perp),\, C_{D0}(0)\big)}
       {C_{D0}(0) - C_{D,\mathrm{ref}}},
  \label{eq:cl}
\end{equation}
where $C_{D0}(0) \approx 0.493$ denotes the subcritical laminar limit and
$C_{D,\mathrm{ref}} \approx 0.242$ is the calibration drag at the reference
anchor point (Reynolds number $3.34\times10^5$, $S_\perp = 0.19$, corresponding
to $v_{\mathrm{ref}} \approx \SI{23.6}{\metre\per\second}$ for kinematic
viscosity $\nu = \SI{1.54e-5}{\metre\squared\per\second}$ and diameter
$D = \SI{0.218}{\metre}$; \citealp{goff2010}). These constants are the calibration
of \citet{kiratidis2018}, who matched the wind-tunnel data of \citet{asai2007} at
this Reynolds number: $C_L = 0.29$ at $S_\perp = 0.19$ and $C_L = 0.33$ at
$S_\perp = 0.31$. The same curve gives $C_L = 0.14$ at $S_\perp = 0.06$, against a
measured 0.15.

It is important to note that \citet{kiratidis2018} did not report experimental
uncertainties or error bounds for their digitized wind-tunnel datasets and
trajectory fits (reproduced in Figure~\ref{fig:coefficients}, Appendix~\ref{app:coef-data}). As they noted,
empirical measurements of sports balls exhibit considerable trial-to-trial
scatter due to unpredictable seam orientations relative to the airflow and
subtle boundary-layer tripping during launch, yet no formal confidence intervals
or error bars accompany their equations. This absence of published uncertainty
bounds directly motivates the sensitivity analyses and Monte Carlo sampling
framework developed in Section~\ref{sec:montecarlo}.

Their model does give different curves for the other World Cup balls they
characterize, and Section~\ref{sec:priors} uses the spread between balls as
the uncertainty of the coefficients.

To check that these coefficients are realistic, we compare them with the
independent wind-tunnel measurements of \citet{kray2014}. They tested an
aluminum model with the 14-panel layout of the Teamgeist and a textured
surface, spun by a motor inside the ball about an axis perpendicular to the
flow, at $0.96\times10^5 < \mathrm{Re} < 4.62\times10^5$. Spin parameters reached
$S_P = 1.12$ at the lowest Reynolds number but only about 0.33 at the
supercritical ones, where the motor's power set the limit. Penalty speeds of
\SIrange{20}{34}{\metre\per\second} correspond to
$\mathrm{Re} \approx 2.9\times10^5$ to $4.9\times10^5$, the supercritical
range of their data, where the boundary layer is turbulent on both sides of
the ball.

Over these speeds Equation~\eqref{eq:cd0} gives a non-spinning drag
coefficient $C_{D0} \approx 0.144$--$0.153$. This matches the Teamgeist data
of \citet{asai2007} that \citet[Fig.~2]{kray2014} reproduce, but is lower than
the $C_D \approx 0.24$ of their own model at $\mathrm{Re} = 3.45\times10^5$,
which they attribute to later boundary-layer separation on the real
Teamgeist. Their model therefore does not replace the real ball's drag, but it
does test the size of the spin terms. At $\mathrm{Re} = 3.42\times10^5$ and
$4.62\times10^5$ their drag coefficient rises by roughly 0.1 between
$S_P = 0$ and $0.2$ \citep[Fig.~9]{kray2014}, against
$\Delta C_D = 0.514\,S_\perp \approx 0.10$ from Equation~\eqref{eq:cd}. Over
the same range their Magnus coefficient climbs to about 0.23--0.27, against
$C_L \approx 0.29$ from Equation~\eqref{eq:cl}, and at high spin it levels off
between 0.30 and 0.35, where Equation~\eqref{eq:cl} gives 0.31--0.33 at
$S_\perp = 0.3$. The Monte Carlo model of Section~\ref{sec:montecarlo} also
lets the lift coefficient fall by up to \SI{25}{\percent} to cover other balls
(Table~\ref{tab:balls}).

Their data also show two effects that our model leaves out. A negative Magnus
force, down to $C_M = -0.09$ at $S_P = 0.12$, appears in the critical range
near $\mathrm{Re} = 2.06\times10^5$, below penalty speeds; at the
supercritical Reynolds numbers $C_M$ increases with spin and the side force
stays near zero. For spin below about \SI{2}{\hertz}, the sideways force
fluctuates instead of following the Magnus law (knuckling), and
\citet{kray2014} recommend their Magnus data only above this rate. This is why
we treat knuckling as a separate force that acts only at low spin
(Section~\ref{sec:variants}).

\subsection{Spin Decay}
\label{sec:spin-decay}

Air friction slows the ball's spin during flight. \citet{tsukada2008} filmed
22 kicks at \SIrange{19}{31}{\metre\per\second} and found that they lost
\SIrange{5}{15}{\percent} of their spin over \SI{18}{\metre}, independent of
the initial spin, with the direction of the spin axis unchanged. We model this
as an exponential decay with the distance $s$ the ball has travelled,
\begin{equation}
  |\bm{\omega}(t)| = |\bm{\omega}_0|\, e^{-s(t)/L}, \qquad \dot{s} = |\bm{v}|,
  \label{eq:spindecay}
\end{equation}
with the axis fixed. This is the form that follows from an aerodynamic
spin-down torque proportional to spin times speed, which
\citet{james2008} measured for a football. The measured losses correspond to a decay
length $L$ between \SI{110}{\metre} and \SI{350}{\metre}. Over the
\SI{11}{\metre} of a penalty the spin therefore falls by only
\SIrange{3}{10}{\percent}.

\subsection{Realistic Spin Range of Kicked Balls}
\label{sec:kick-spin}

Two studies have filmed kicked balls with high-speed cameras and measured
their spin. \citet{tsukada2011} recorded ten kicks by each of two players.
Instep kicks spun at $3.2 \pm 0.7$ and $3.3 \pm 1.0$\,\si{\rev\per\second}
(mean $\pm$ standard deviation for each player), mostly as backspin, with the
axis within about \SI{25}{\degree} of pure backspin. In-front kicks, which
curl the ball, spun faster, at $7.5 \pm 0.9$ and
$7.2 \pm 1.1$\,\si{\rev\per\second}, mostly as sidespin, with the axis
\SIrange{98}{108}{\degree} from backspin and some screw spin.
\citet{whiteside2010} found $3.6 \pm 0.7$\,\si{\rev\per\second} for straight
kicks and $5.8 \pm 0.8$\,\si{\rev\per\second} for curved ones.

We take the range of measured kicks to be spin rates up to
\SI{9.3}{\rev\per\second}, the mean plus two standard deviations of the
fastest kicks ($7.5 + 2 \times 0.9$), with the spin axis up to
\SI{110}{\degree} from pure backspin toward sidespin in either direction. The
rate bound covers about \SI{98}{\percent} of those kicks if their rates are
normally distributed, though not every kick: the fastest of the 22 kicks
filmed by \citet{tsukada2008} spun at \SI{10.1}{\rev\per\second}. The angle
bound covers both kick types, curling either way, and stops at
\SI{110}{\degree} because neither study found strong topspin. At the
\SI{32}{\metre\per\second} of our scenario, \SI{9.3}{\rev\per\second}
corresponds to $S_\perp \approx 0.20$ at launch.

\subsection{Solving the Equations}
\label{sec:integration}

Equations~\eqref{eq:accel}--\eqref{eq:spindecay} have no closed-form solution,
so we integrate them numerically with the adaptive eighth-order Runge--Kutta
method DOP853 \citep{hairer1993}, as implemented in SciPy \citep{scipy2020}.
The state is the position, velocity and distance travelled. The integration
starts at $\bm{r}_0 = (0, 0, 0.11)$ with launch velocity $\bm{v}_0$ and stops
when the ball's center reaches the goal plane $x = 11$, which the solver
locates as an event.

The method has no fixed time step. At each step it estimates its own error
and chooses the next step so that the error stays below a set tolerance. We
use a relative tolerance of $10^{-9}$ and an absolute tolerance of
$10^{-11}$ for every result we report, and the looser $10^{-7}$ and $10^{-9}$
inside the fitting loops of Section~\ref{sec:search}, where many trajectories
are integrated. Because the forces vary smoothly, the solver needs only four
or five steps to reach the goal: a first step of about \SI{0.4}{\milli\second},
then steps of up to about \SI{0.3}{\second}. Positions at the observation
times and at the goal plane come from the method's built-in high-order
interpolation between steps \citep{hairer1993}. To confirm that these long
steps are accurate, we compared trajectories with a reference integration at
a relative tolerance of $10^{-13}$. The crossing points differ by less than
\SI{1}{\nano\metre} at our standard tolerance and by less than
\SI{2}{\micro\metre} at the looser one, far below any other source of error.

We also checked the solver against cases with exact solutions: flight under
gravity alone, drag alone in one dimension, and lift alone, for which the ball
moves in a circle at constant speed. In each case the numerical and exact
trajectories agree to better than \SI{1e-6}{\metre}.

\subsection{Assumptions}
\label{sec:assumptions}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{The ball is a rigid sphere of radius \SI{0.11}{\metre}.}
    The problem places the ball's center \SI{0.11}{\metre} above the ground,
    and the Laws of the Game require a circumference of
    \SIrange{68}{70}{\centi\metre}, a radius of
    \SIrange{10.8}{11.1}{\centi\metre} \citep{ifab2025}.
  \item \textbf{The ball leaves the foot at $t = 0$ from $\bm{r}_0$.} This is
    how the problem defines the kick. After $t = 0$ the foot no longer touches
    the ball.
  \item \textbf{Only gravity, drag and Magnus lift act on the ball.} Initially, here and
    in Section~\ref{sec:exact-fits}, we neglect buoyancy, which is
    \SI{1.6}{\percent} of the ball's weight, and the added mass of the air the
    ball carries with it; together they change its height at the goal by about
    \SI{1}{\centi\metre}. We also neglect the random sideways forces that act
    on balls kicked with almost no spin (``knuckling''), which are strongest
    below about \SI{1}{\rev\per\second} \citep{mizota2013}.
    Section~\ref{sec:variants} adds both effects.
  \item \textbf{The air is still.} We take the match to be played without
    wind, as indoors or on a calm day. Wind is a property of the venue, not
    of the kick, and two observations in the first \SI{0.1}{\second} could
    not reveal it: over that time it changes the ball's path far less than
    the rounding of the data. Where wind matters, it would have to be
    measured and given to the model as an input.
  \item \textbf{The ball has the drag and lift characteristics of the Teamgeist ball.} Balls with
    different panel designs have different drag and lift curves
    \citep{kiratidis2018}. Here and in Section~\ref{sec:exact-fits} we use the
    Teamgeist curves; Section~\ref{sec:montecarlo} covers the other balls by
    scaling both coefficients (Section~\ref{sec:priors}).
  \item \textbf{The spin axis keeps its direction and the spin rate decays
    with distance} as in Equation~\eqref{eq:spindecay} \citep{tsukada2008}.
  \item \textbf{The flight ends at the goal plane.} We do not model the ball
    hitting a post, the crossbar or the ground. The crossing point is that of
    the ball's center, as the problem defines it.
\end{enumerate}

In this section and the next we also hold the ball mass and spin-decay
length fixed at $m = \SI{0.43}{\kilo\gram}$ (the middle of the legal range
\SIrange{0.41}{0.45}{\kilo\gram}, \citealp{ifab2025}) and
$L = \SI{230}{\metre}$ (the middle of the measured range);
Section~\ref{sec:montecarlo} lets them vary. We treat the air density as
known, since a goalkeeper might theoretically be able to know the conditions on the day. Here and in
Section~\ref{sec:exact-fits} we use $\rho = \SI{1.20}{\kilo\gram\per\metre\cubed}$;
Section~\ref{sec:montecarlo} uses the sea-level value of the standard
atmosphere, \SI{1.225}{\kilo\gram\per\metre\cubed} \citep{ussa1976}. A change in
density of this size moves the predicted crossing point by less than
\SI{2.5}{\centi\metre} (Section~\ref{sec:fixed-results}).

% =====================================================================
\section{Exact Fits in the Well-Constrained Case}
\label{sec:exact-fits}
% =====================================================================

With external variables like the air density, ball mass and spin-decay length fixed, a trajectory is
determined by six numbers: the three components of the launch velocity
$\bm{v}_0$ and the three components of the spin $\bm{\omega}_0$. The two
observations also supply six numbers. In this section we ask which launch
velocities and spins reproduce both observations to within their rounding,
and where those trajectories cross the goal plane. This general method applies to any
pair of observations; here we apply it to the scenario of
Table~\ref{tab:scenario}.

\subsection{Spin Components}
\label{sec:spin-frame}

We describe the spin relative to the direction of launch. Let
$\hat{\bm{f}} = \bm{v}_0/|\bm{v}_0|$ point along the launch velocity, let
$\hat{\bm{e}}_r = \hat{\bm{f}}\times\hat{\bm{z}}/|\hat{\bm{f}}\times\hat{\bm{z}}|$
point horizontally to the right of the flight, and let
$\hat{\bm{e}}_u = \hat{\bm{e}}_r\times\hat{\bm{f}}$ point up. We write
\begin{equation}
  \bm{\omega}_0 = \omega_b\,\hat{\bm{e}}_r + \omega_s\,\hat{\bm{e}}_u
                + \omega_f\,\hat{\bm{f}},
  \label{eq:spinframe}
\end{equation}
where $\omega_b$ is backspin (positive values lift the ball, negative values
are topspin), $\omega_s$ is sidespin (positive values curl the ball to the
kicker's left, $+y$) and $\omega_f$ is screw spin, rotation about the
direction of travel like the spiral of a thrown American football. We quote spin rates in revolutions per second
(\si{\rev\per\second}).

\subsection{Consistency with the data}
\label{sec:consistency}

The observed positions are given to the nearest centimeter, so each true
coordinate lies within \SI{5}{\milli\metre} of the stated value. Let
$\bm{r}(t;\, \bm{v}_0, \omega_b, \omega_s)$ be the model's position and
$\tilde{\bm{r}}_1$, $\tilde{\bm{r}}_2$ the observed positions at $t_1$ and
$t_2$. We call a trajectory \emph{consistent} with the data if all six
coordinate misses are at most \SI{5}{\milli\metre}:
\begin{equation}
  \delta_{\max} \;=\; \max_{i = 1,2}\;
  \big\| \bm{r}(t_i;\, \bm{v}_0, \omega_b, \omega_s) - \tilde{\bm{r}}_i \big\|_\infty
  \;\le\; \SI{5}{\milli\metre},
  \label{eq:consistent}
\end{equation}
where $\|\cdot\|_\infty$ is the largest of the three coordinate differences.
This is a hard bound rather than a statistical one, because rounding error
can never exceed half the last digit.

\subsection{Finding every consistent trajectory}
\label{sec:search}

\label{sec:screw}
Screw spin $\omega_f$, rotation about the direction of travel
(Equation~\ref{eq:spinframe}), barely affects the observed part of the
flight, so we set $\omega_f = 0$ and search over backspin and sidespin only.\footnote{Screw spin
gives no lift at launch, since its axis lies along the velocity and
$\bm{\omega}\times\bm{v} = \bm{0}$. It acts only through the angle $\phi$ that
opens between the spin axis and the velocity as the path bends, as a
perpendicular spin of $\omega_f \sin\phi$. For the best fit below, $\phi$ is
only \SI{1.3}{\degree} at $t_1$ and \SI{2.6}{\degree} at $t_2$, and adding up
to \SI{30}{\rev\per\second} of screw spin changes its misses by less than
\SI{0.5}{\milli\metre}. With screw spin free, the six unknowns match the six
observed coordinates in number and exact fits exist, but every one we found
(by least squares from several starting points) needs nearly
\SI{1000}{\rev\per\second} of screw spin, over a hundred times the spin of
measured kicks \citep{tsukada2011, whiteside2010}.}
This leaves five unknowns for six observed coordinates, so in general no
trajectory passes exactly through both observations, and we look instead for
every trajectory that is consistent with them.
The data fix the launch velocity sharply but the spin only weakly, so we
treat the two differently: we search over spin, and for each spin we solve
for the launch velocity.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Starting guess.} The constant-acceleration curve through
    $\bm{r}_0$, $\tilde{\bm{r}}_1$ and $\tilde{\bm{r}}_2$ has initial velocity
    \begin{equation}
      \bm{v}_{\mathrm{init}} = \frac{4\tilde{\bm{r}}_1 - \tilde{\bm{r}}_2 - 3\bm{r}_0}{2\,\Delta t},
      \qquad \Delta t = \SI{0.05}{\second},
      \label{eq:vinit}
    \end{equation}
    which for the scenario is $(30.4, 9.4, 6.9)$\,\si{\metre\per\second}.
  \item \textbf{Fit the launch velocity.} For a given spin
    $(\omega_b, \omega_s)$, we find the $\bm{v}_0$ that minimizes the sum of
    the six squared misses, using the Levenberg--Marquardt algorithm
    \citep{levenberg1944, marquardt1963, more1978} from
    $\bm{v}_{\mathrm{init}}$. Each evaluation integrates the equations of
    motion to $t_2$. The derivatives of the positions with respect to
    $\bm{v}_0$ are computed by central differences, integrating the
    perturbed trajectories together with the main one so that all of them
    use the same time steps.
  \item \textbf{Minimize the worst miss.} The least-squares fit makes the
    misses small on average, but Equation~\eqref{eq:consistent} limits the
    largest one. Near the least-squares solution the misses depend almost
    linearly on $\bm{v}_0$, so we find the change in $\bm{v}_0$ that
    minimizes the largest miss by solving a small linear program
    \citep{huangfu2018}. We then integrate the corrected trajectory again and
    keep the correction only if it reduces $\delta_{\max}$. 
  \item \textbf{Scan the spin.} We repeat steps 2--3 on a grid of spins,
    $\omega_b$ and $\omega_s$ each from \SIrange{-40}{40}{\rev\per\second} in
    steps of \SI{0.5}{\rev\per\second}, a total of 25{,}921 spins. The
    consistent spins lie well inside the grid, so the grid does not cut off
    any solutions.
  \item \textbf{Fly each trajectory to the goal.} For every grid point we
    integrate the trajectory to $x = 11$ and record the crossing point.
\end{enumerate}
As an independent check on step 4, we also minimized the sum of squared
misses over all five unknowns at once, from 200 random starting spins of up to
\SI{30}{\rev\per\second}. Of the 200 starts, 162 converged to the same
minimum, next to the best grid point. The other 38 stopped on a ring of
spins near \SI{30}{\rev\per\second}. There $S_\perp \approx 0.64$, the drag in
Equation~\eqref{eq:cd} approaches its low-speed value and the lift of
Equation~\eqref{eq:cl} almost vanishes, so the direction of the spin no longer
matters. This ring is an edge of the lift model rather than a separate
solution, and none of its points is consistent with the data.

\subsection{Fixed-Parameter Results}
\label{sec:fixed-lessons}

This analysis gives the crossing point implied by the model if the
observations are exact up to rounding. It also exposes three limits that
motivate the Monte Carlo method of the next section.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{The data determine the spin only weakly.} The extra curvature
    that spin must explain is about \SI{1}{\centi\metre}, the size of the
    rounding itself. When we moved the observations randomly within their
    rounding and refitted 40 times, the preferred spin ranged from
    \SIrange{16}{26}{\rev\per\second}. The spin estimate is therefore driven
    as much by how the numbers were rounded as by the physics.
  \item \textbf{The data favor spins at the top of the measured range.}
    Measured instep and curled kicks average \SIrange{3}{7.5}{\rev\per\second},
    and the fastest reach about \SI{9.3}{\rev\per\second}
    (Section~\ref{sec:kick-spin}). Only spins at that upper edge fit the
    data, and only just (\SI{4.9}{\milli\metre} against the
    \SI{5}{\milli\metre} bound). The best fit spins at
    \SI{22.7}{\rev\per\second}, more than twice as fast as any measured kick.
    Either this kick spun unusually fast, or the rounding happens to
    exaggerate the curvature, as the previous point suggests is possible.
  \item \textbf{The fits rely on the lift model beyond its data.} The
    consistent spins correspond to $S_\perp$ between $0.20$ and $0.60$, and
    \SI{93}{\percent} of them lie above $S_\perp = 0.31$, the largest spin
    parameter in the wind-tunnel data behind Equation~\eqref{eq:cl}. The exact
    location of the consistent region therefore depends on an extrapolated
    part of the lift curve.
\end{itemize}
The Monte Carlo method addresses these limits. It draws the spin only from
the range of measured kicks and lets the observations decide which spins in
that range are most likely; this also keeps $S_\perp$ below about 0.23
throughout the flight, inside the range of the lift data. It replaces the hard \SI{5}{\milli\metre} bound with
a measurement error of the same spread, so each draw is graded by how well it
fits rather than being simply accepted or rejected. The Monte Carlo model also samples the ball's mass, spin
decay and drag and lift coefficients, so that the stated uncertainty includes
everything the observations cannot determine.

% =====================================================================
\section{Monte Carlo Model}
\label{sec:montecarlo}
% =====================================================================

Section~\ref{sec:exact-fits} found the trajectories that fit the observations
under one fixed set of assumptions: the drag and lift characteristics of the Teamgeist, a ball of
average mass, a typical spin decay, and observations exact to their rounding.
For a more detailed general solution, we don't know any of these features exactly, and the spin is known only to lie somewhere in
the range of measured kicks. The Monte Carlo method asks how uncertain the
predicted crossing point is once these assumptions are themselves uncertain.

We describe each uncertain input by a probability distribution, based on
measurements where they exist. Each sample, or \emph{draw}, picks a value for
every input, finds the launch velocity that best explains the observations,
and flies the ball to the goal. Draws whose inputs explain the observations
well count for more. The spread of the weighted crossing points reflects the
uncertainty of the prediction. A
different choice of distributions would give a different spread, so
Section~\ref{sec:results} also tests how the answer changes when the input
distributions are modified.

We first extend the physical model (Section~\ref{sec:variants}), then give the
distributions of the uncertain inputs (Section~\ref{sec:priors}), and finally
describe how the draws are made and combined (Sections~\ref{sec:draw}
and~\ref{sec:summaries}).

\subsection{Extending the Model}
\label{sec:variants}

We extend the model of Section~\ref{sec:simple-model} with two more effects:
buoyancy and the added mass of the surrounding air, and knuckling. Each is
small for the kick specified in the problem. Differences in aerodynamics between balls are also added to the model and  treated as
uncertainty in the drag and lift coefficients (Section~\ref{sec:priors}).

The ball displaces a mass of air
$\rho V = \SI{6.8}{\gram}$, where $V = \tfrac{4}{3}\pi r^3$, about
\SI{1.6}{\percent} of its own mass. Buoyancy reduces its weight by $\rho V g$.
Accelerating the ball also accelerates the air around it, which adds half the
displaced mass to its inertia but not to its weight \citep{batchelor1967}.
With both effects, Equation~\eqref{eq:eom} becomes
\begin{equation}
  \left(m + \tfrac{1}{2}\rho V\right) \dot{\bm{v}}
  = (m - \rho V)\,\bm{g} + \bm{F}_D + \bm{F}_L .
  \label{eq:eom-air}
\end{equation}

A ball kicked with little spin can swerve erratically
as its wake sheds unevenly \citep{mizota2013}. Our model of this force follows
the flight measurements of \citet{mizota2013}. For balls launched by machine
at \SI{22.8}{} and \SI{29.2}{\metre\per\second} with about
\SI{1}{\rev\per\second} of spin, the sideways force coefficient they measured
varied roughly sinusoidally in time, with an amplitude of 0.10--0.15 and a
period of \SI{1.3}{\second} (their Fig.~1e,h). In their wind tunnel the same
force fluctuated more randomly, at \SIrange{0.7}{2.5}{\hertz}. We therefore
model knuckling as a sinusoidally oscillating sideways force,
\begin{equation}
  \bm{F}_k = \tfrac{1}{2}\rho A\, C_k\, |\bm{v}|^2\,
  \min\!\left(1, \frac{\omega_c}{|\bm{\omega}|}\right)
  \sin(2\pi f t + \phi)\, \hat{\bm{k}},
  \label{eq:knuckle}
\end{equation}
where $\hat{\bm{k}}$ is a unit vector perpendicular to the launch velocity.
Its direction around the flight path and the phase $\phi$ are drawn uniformly
in each draw. We take the peak coefficient $C_k = 0.10$, the low end of both
the amplitudes of \citet{mizota2013} and the forces \citet{hong2010} measured on kicked knuckleballs (about \SI{2}{\newton}
at \SIrange{20}{30}{\metre\per\second}), and the average frequency of
\citet{hong2010}, $f = \SI{3.5}{\hertz}$. The force is at full strength up to
$\omega_c = \SI{1}{\rev\per\second}$, the spin at which \citet{mizota2013}
still observed knuckling, and weakens in proportion to $1/|\bm{\omega}|$ above
it, because faster spin replaces the unsteady force with a steady Magnus force
by about \SIrange{2}{3}{\rev\per\second} \citep{mizota2013}. Where \citet{mizota2013} and \citet{hong2010} differ, we take
the value that moves the ball less. For the amplitude, we take the lowest
value that either study reports. For the frequency, we take the
\SI{3.5}{\hertz} of \citet{hong2010} over the \SI{0.8}{\hertz} that
\citet{mizota2013} measured in flight. A faster oscillation reverses
direction more often during the \SI{0.4}{\second} flight, so its pushes
largely cancel and it moves the ball less. The model is
therefore more likely to understate the final impact of knuckling than to overstate it.

We run three versions of the model on the same random draws, so that
differences between them are not Monte Carlo noise. The \emph{basic} model
has gravity, drag and lift (Equations~\eqref{eq:accel}
and~\eqref{eq:factors}) with constant spin. The \emph{standard} model adds
spin decay (Equation~\eqref{eq:spindecay}), buoyancy and added mass
(Equation~\eqref{eq:eom-air}). The \emph{extended} model adds the knuckling
force of Equation~\eqref{eq:knuckle} to the standard model. All three assume
still air (Section~\ref{sec:assumptions}).

\subsection{Uncertain Inputs and Their Distributions}
\label{sec:priors}

Table~\ref{tab:priors} lists the inputs sampled in each draw, written together
as $\bm{\theta}$. We use uniform distributions
between bounds taken from the literature, because the sources report a handful
of kicks or balls rather than a distribution, and because a uniform range
makes each assumption easy to state and to test.

\paragraph{Spin.} We use the launch-frame components of
Section~\ref{sec:spin-frame}. Section~\ref{sec:exact-fits} set the screw spin
to zero; here we sample it, because curled kicks carry a noticeable amount.
Writing $q$ for the share of the spin axis that lies along the direction of
travel, the spin is
\begin{equation}
  \omega_b = |\bm{\omega}_0| \sqrt{1 - q^2}\, \cos\psi, \qquad
  \omega_s = |\bm{\omega}_0| \sqrt{1 - q^2}\, \sin\psi, \qquad
  \omega_f = |\bm{\omega}_0|\, q,
  \label{eq:spinprior}
\end{equation}
so $|\bm{\omega}_0|$ is the total spin rate, $\psi = 0$ is pure backspin and
$\psi = \pm\SI{90}{\degree}$ is pure sidespin, with positive values curling
the ball to the kicker's left.

The rate and the angle are uniform over the range of measured kicks
(Section~\ref{sec:kick-spin}). We do not specify a kick type: the uniform rate
and angle cover instep and curled kicks, curling either way, without favoring
any of them. The screw share is uniform between $-0.52$ and $0.52$.
\citet{tsukada2011} measured screw shares of $0.08 \pm 0.09$ and
$-0.11 \pm 0.08$ for instep kicks and $-0.38 \pm 0.05$ and $-0.42 \pm 0.05$
for in-front kicks. The bound is the largest mean plus two standard
deviations, the same rule as for the rate, and the range is symmetric
because the sign depends on the kicking foot.

\paragraph{Spin decay.} The decay length $L$ of Equation~\eqref{eq:spindecay}
is uniform between \SI{110}{\metre} and \SI{350}{\metre}, the range measured by
\citet{tsukada2008} (Section~\ref{sec:spin-decay}). Over the \SI{11}{\metre}
of a penalty this is a loss of \SIrange{3}{10}{\percent} of the spin.

\paragraph{Ball mass.} The mass is uniform over the legal range,
\SIrange{0.41}{0.45}{\kilo\gram} \citep{ifab2025}. A ball's mass changes its
response to drag and lift by only a few percent, and the observations cannot
tell one mass from another.

\paragraph{Drag and lift factors.} To let the ball's aerodynamics differ from
the Teamgeist's, each draw multiplies the drag and lift coefficients of
Equations~\eqref{eq:cd} and~\eqref{eq:cl} by constant factors,
\begin{equation}
  C_D \;\to\; f_D\, C_D(v, S_\perp), \qquad
  C_L \;\to\; f_L\, C_L(v, S_\perp).
  \label{eq:factors}
\end{equation}
The factor $f_D$ multiplies the drag force only. The drag coefficient inside
the lift model (Equation~\eqref{eq:cl}) keeps its published value, so the two
factors act independently.

The ranges come from how much the coefficients differ between balls.
Table~\ref{tab:balls} compares the other
three World Cup balls in \citet{kiratidis2018} with the Teamgeist, using their
own equations (their Table~1 and Equations~5--9) over the speeds and spin
parameters of a penalty kick. The Teamgeist has the lowest drag and the most
lift of the four; the other balls have up to \SI{34}{\percent} more drag and up
to \SI{25}{\percent} less lift. Each factor runs from the Teamgeist value of 1
to the most extreme ball, rounded outward. Independently, \citet{carre2002}
measured lift coefficients \SIrange{20}{29}{\percent} below the Teamgeist
curve, at somewhat higher spin parameters, within the same spread.

less lift. Drawing the two factors independently ignores this link and allows
combinations no real ball has, which can only widen the predicted spread.

\paragraph{Air density.} The air density variation is not sampled. We fix it at
$\rho = \SI{1.225}{\kilo\gram\per\metre\cubed}$, as explained in
Section~\ref{sec:assumptions}.

\paragraph{Measurement error.} The observed coordinates are rounded to the
nearest centimeter. A rounding error spread uniformly over
$\pm\SI{5}{\milli\metre}$ has standard deviation
$\SI{1}{\centi\metre}/\sqrt{12} = \SI{2.9}{\milli\metre}$. We model each
observed coordinate as the true position plus an independent Gaussian error
with this standard deviation, $\sigma = \SI{2.9}{\milli\metre}$. Unlike the
hard bound of Section~\ref{sec:consistency}, a Gaussian error lets us grade
how well each draw fits rather than only accept or reject it.
Section~\ref{sec:results} repeats the analysis with larger $\sigma$, as for a
realistic tracking system.

\subsection{Propagating Uncertainty}
\label{sec:draw}

Each draw carries one set of inputs through the model to a crossing point, in
five steps.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Sample $\bm{\theta}$} from Table~\ref{tab:priors}.
  \item \textbf{Fit the launch velocity.} Find the $\bm{v}_0^*$ that
    minimizes
    \begin{equation}
      \chi^2(\bm{v}_0) = \sum_{i=1}^{2}
        \frac{\big\|\bm{r}(t_i;\, \bm{v}_0, \bm{\theta}) - \tilde{\bm{r}}_i\big\|^2}{\sigma^2},
      \label{eq:chi2}
    \end{equation}
    the sum of the six squared misses in units of $\sigma$, by the
    Levenberg--Marquardt method of Section~\ref{sec:search}. Call the minimum
    $\chi^2_{\min}$.
  \item \textbf{Weight the draw} by
    \begin{equation}
      w = e^{-\chi^2_{\min}/2},
      \label{eq:weight}
    \end{equation}
    the likelihood of the observations under this draw's inputs.
  \item \textbf{Sample the launch velocity} from a normal distribution
    centered on $\bm{v}_0^*$ with covariance $(J^\mathsf{T}J)^{-1}$, where $J$
    is the $6 \times 3$ matrix of derivatives of the six misses (divided by
    $\sigma$) with respect to $\bm{v}_0$. This is the uncertainty that the
    measurement error alone leaves in the launch velocity, about
    \SI{0.03}{\metre\per\second} in each component.
  \item \textbf{Fly the ball} from $\bm{r}_0$ with this launch velocity until
    its center reaches $x = 11$, and record the crossing point $(y, z)$ and
    whether the shot is on target, wide or over.
\end{enumerate}
Every draw has its own random seed, so the results are reproducible and
independent of how the draws are split across processors. We use
$N = 100{,}000$ draws per run.

By Bayes' theorem,
the probability of $\bm{\theta}$ and $\bm{v}_0$ given the observations $d$ is
proportional to the probability of the observations given them, times the
prior probability of $\bm{\theta}$:
\begin{equation}
  p(\bm{\theta}, \bm{v}_0 \mid d) \;\propto\;
  e^{-\chi^2(\bm{v}_0;\, \bm{\theta})/2}\; p(\bm{\theta}).
  \label{eq:bayes}
\end{equation}
We take all launch velocities to be equally likely beforehand, since the
observations fix $\bm{v}_0$ far more tightly than any prior knowledge could.
Step~1 samples $\bm{\theta}$ from $p(\bm{\theta})$, and the weight of step~3
supplies the remaining factor. This is importance sampling with the prior as
the proposal \citep{robert2004}. Near its minimum $\chi^2$ is close to
quadratic in $\bm{v}_0$, so for a given $\bm{\theta}$ the launch velocity is
approximately normal with the mean and covariance of step~4. Integrating that
normal over $\bm{v}_0$ would add a factor $|J^\mathsf{T}J|^{-1/2}$ to the
weight \citep{tierney1986}. We omit this since it varies by at most about
\SI{5}{\percent} across draws, because the launch velocity is set mainly by
the positions and times rather than by the physics.

The spread of the weights measures how much the observations narrow down
$\bm{\theta}$. We report the effective sample size
\begin{equation}
  N_{\mathrm{eff}} = \frac{\left(\sum_n w_n\right)^2}{\sum_n w_n^2},
  \label{eq:ess}
\end{equation}
which is $N$ when every draw fits equally well and approaches 1 when a single
draw dominates \citep{kish1965}.

\subsection{From Draws to a Prediction}
\label{sec:summaries}

The weighted crossing points sample the probability distribution of where
the ball crosses the goal plane. We estimate its density with a weighted
Gaussian kernel density estimate, with the bandwidth set by Scott's rule
using $N_{\mathrm{eff}}$ in place of $N$ \citep{scott1992}.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Most likely crossing point.} The peak of the density, found on
    a $120 \times 120$ grid and refined with the Nelder--Mead method
    \citep{nelder1965}.
  \item \textbf{Uncertainty regions.} The 68\% and 95\% highest-density
    regions: the smallest regions that contain that fraction of the
    probability. Each is the set of points where the density exceeds a
    threshold, chosen so that the draws above it carry 68\% or 95\% of the
    total weight \citep{hyndman1996}.
  \item \textbf{Spread.} The weighted mean and standard deviation of $y$ and
    $z$.
  \item \textbf{Outcome probabilities.} The weighted fractions of draws that
    are on target, wide or over.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Effect of Measurement Precision}
\label{sec:sigma}

The sharpness of the prediction depends mainly on how precisely the ball's
position is known (Table~\ref{tab:sigma}, Figure~\ref{fig:predicted}). The
curvature that reveals the sidespin is about \SI{1}{\centi\metre} over the
first \SI{0.1}{\second} (Section~\ref{sec:fixed-lessons}). With
$\sigma = \SI{2.9}{\milli\metre}$ that curvature is measured. With
$\sigma = \SI{1}{\centi\metre}$ it is only partly resolved, and the region
spreads toward the post and the crossbar. With $\sigma = \SI{2}{\centi\metre}$
it is lost in the noise: the weights barely change the inputs
($N_{\mathrm{eff}}$ is 95\% of $N$), the most likely point moves to
$(3.20, 2.00)$\,\si{\metre}, close to where a ball without spin would cross,
and the spread is set almost entirely by the spin distribution. A real
tracking system or a goalkeeper's eye is closer to the larger values
than to the rounding of the given data, so in practice the uncertainty is
likely to be several times larger than in Equation~\eqref{eq:answer}.

\subsection{Effect of the Physics and the Input Distributions}
\label{sec:sensitivity}

The three model variants give nearly the same answer. At
$\sigma = \SI{2.9}{\milli\metre}$ the most likely point is
$(2.60, 1.93)$\,\si{\metre} for the basic model, $(2.59, 1.95)$ for the
standard model and $(2.59, 1.96)$ for the extended model, with spreads that
agree to within a few millimeters. Buoyancy, added mass, spin decay and
knuckling are all real, but for this kick none of them moves the prediction
by more than \SI{2}{\centi\metre}.

To see how much the answer depends on the input distributions, we changed

\SIrange{7}{14}{\centi\metre} across. These shifts are comparable to the
stated spread, so the lift curve and the spin range are the assumptions that
most deserve better data.

\subsection{Comparison with the Exact Fits}
\label{sec:compare}

The Monte Carlo answer agrees with Section~\ref{sec:exact-fits}. Its most
likely point lies \SI{9}{\centi\metre} from the best exact fit at
$(2.68, 1.92)$\,\si{\metre}, and between that fit and the crossing of the
fastest measured spins near $(2.5, 2.2)$\,\si{\metre}. Its spread is
smaller than the range of consistent crossings in Table~\ref{tab:fixed}
(\SI{0.8}{\metre} by \SI{0.6}{\metre}), because the Monte Carlo draws spin
only from the range of measured kicks, up to \SI{9.3}{\rev\per\second},
while the consistent fits reach \SI{28}{\rev\per\second}. Both methods
place the ball in the same corner, about a meter inside the post.

\subsection{Testing on Simulated Kicks}
\label{sec:validation}

We check that the stated uncertainty is honest: the true crossing point
should land inside the 68\% region about 68\% of the time, and inside the
95\% region about 95\% of the time. The true crossing of the given kick is
unknown, so we simulated 200 kicks whose crossings we know. Each had its
spin, ball and mass drawn from the distributions of
Section~\ref{sec:priors}, a launch speed between \SI{20}{} and
\SI{34}{\metre\per\second}, and a random aim on or around the goal. We gave
the method only the ball's positions at \SI{0.05}{} and \SI{0.10}{\second},
rounded to the nearest centimeter as in the problem.


The true crossing landed inside the 68\% region for 68\% of the kicks and
inside the 95\% region for 98.5\%; with 200 kicks, each of these fractions
is uncertain by about $\pm 0.03$. The stated uncertainty is therefore
accurate, and if anything slightly cautious, even though the data are
rounded while the method assumes Gaussian errors. The most likely point
misses the true crossing by \SI{18}{\centi\metre} in a typical kick. Because the simulated kicks
follow our own model, this shows that the method is consistent with its
assumptions, not that the assumptions match real football; we test that
next.

\subsection{Testing on Real Kicks}
\label{sec:realdata}

To see how the method does on real kicks, we used ball-tracking data from a
match recorded at the Nuremberg football stadium with a radio-based system
that locates a sensor inside the ball about 2{,}000 times a second
\citep{debs2013}. Our copy of the data covers only the first 8.6 minutes of
play, and only two kicks in it reach a goal line untouched. We therefore
used a different target. For every kick that flew freely through the air
for \SI{0.38}{\second}, about the flight time of the penalty, we placed an
imaginary plane across its path where the ball was at that time, between
\SI{6}{} and \SI{9}{\metre} from the kick. Seventeen kicks qualified, at
\SIrange{20}{31}{\metre\per\second}. As for the penalty, the method saw only
the ball's position at the start (\SI{0.02}{\second} after the foot struck
it) and \SI{0.05}{} and \SI{0.10}{\second} later, and predicted where it
would cross the plane. From the scatter of the tracks, the tracking error is
about \SI{0.8}{\centi\metre} horizontally and \SI{2.5}{\centi\metre}
vertically, and we used these values for $\sigma$. Figure~\ref{fig:realkicks}(a)
shows these steps on one kick, and Appendix~\ref{app:realdata} gives the full
procedure and the code.

Sideways, the method works on real kicks as it does on simulated ones
(Table~\ref{tab:realdata}): the typical miss is \SI{14}{\centi\metre}, and
16 of the 17 kicks land within two predicted standard deviations. In height
it is less accurate and overconfident: the typical miss is
\SI{24}{\centi\metre}, and only 59\% of kicks land within two standard
deviations, so the 68\% and 95\% regions contain the true crossing for only
35\% and 59\% of kicks. The likely cause is the tracking system rather than
the physics. Its height readings are three times noisier than its
horizontal ones, and the error drifts slowly rather than varying
independently from one reading to the next. A drift between the two
observations looks like a wrong vertical speed, which grows to tens of
centimeters by the plane, while our error model assumes independent errors.

The test is small, its kicks are passes and shots in open play rather than
penalties, and the true crossing is measured by the same noisy system. We
read it as evidence that the method transfers to real kicks for the curl,
and that a real application would need an error model that matches the
tracking system, especially in height.

\section{Real-Kick Test Procedure}
\label{app:realdata}
% =====================================================================

This appendix describes the test of Section~\ref{sec:realdata} step by step.
Listing~\ref{lst:realdata} shows the core of the code.

\paragraph{Data.} The DEBS 2013 data \citep{debs2013} list one sensor reading
per line: sensor number, time in picoseconds, position $(x, y, z)$ in
millimeters, and the speed and acceleration magnitudes estimated by the
tracker. Our copy of the file is cut off after 8.6 minutes of play, giving
8.5 million readings, 4.2 million of them from ball sensors. Three ball
sensors report about 2{,}000 times a second; every other sensor reports
about 200 times a second. For each kick we keep every fourth ball reading,
one every \SI{2}{\milli\second}.

\paragraph{Finding kicks.} A kick is a moment when the ball's reported speed
rises past \SI{12}{\metre\per\second} and gains at least
\SI{6}{\metre\per\second} within \SI{0.05}{\second} on either side. We time
the strike by the largest reported acceleration in the \SI{0.05}{\second}
before the speed crosses \SI{12}{\metre\per\second}, and treat candidates
less than \SI{0.5}{\second} apart as one kick. This finds 121 kicks.

\paragraph{Choosing kicks.} The test starts \SI{0.02}{\second} after the
strike, once the ball has left the foot, and runs for \SI{0.38}{\second}. A
kick is kept if, over that time, the ball stays above the ground, rises
above \SI{0.5}{\metre} at some point, travels at least \SI{5}{\metre}
horizontally, and never changes its reported speed by more than
\SI{2}{\metre\per\second} between readings, which would mean a touch, block
or bounce. Seventeen kicks pass. The tracker's reported speed overshoots for
a few hundredths of a second after the strike (Figure~\ref{fig:realkicks}),
so we use it only to find and screen kicks; every position the method sees
comes from the tracked positions.

\paragraph{Coordinates.} The starting point $\bm{r}_0$ is a quadratic fit to
the positions within $\pm\SI{0.01}{\second}$ of the start, evaluated at the
start, to reduce noise in a point the method treats as exact. The $x$ axis
points along the horizontal direction from $\bm{r}_0$ to the position
\SI{0.10}{\second} later, $y$ points to the kicker's left and $z$ up. The
plane is where the ball is after \SI{0.38}{\second}, at a distance $D$ along
$x$; shifting the frame so that the plane is at $x = 11$, as in the problem,
puts the start at $(11 - D, 0, z_0)$.

\paragraph{Observations, truth and noise.} The two observations are the
positions \SI{0.05}{} and \SI{0.10}{\second} after the start, interpolated
between readings, and the true crossing is the position at
\SI{0.38}{\second}. For $\sigma$ we fit a smooth (cubic) curve to each
track over the test and take the scatter of the readings about it, in each
direction; the medians over the 17 kicks are \SI{0.8}{}, \SI{0.8}{} and
\SI{2.5}{\centi\metre}.

\paragraph{Prediction.} Each kick is predicted with the standard model and
4{,}000 draws, exactly as for the penalty, using these three values of
$\sigma$.

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Core of the real-kick test. The arrays t, pos, speed and acc hold one ball's readings in seconds, meters, meters per second and meters per second squared.}, label=lst:realdata, captionpos=b]
# Finding kicks
up = np.flatnonzero((speed[1:] >= 12) & (speed[:-1] < 12)) + 1
for i in up:
    lo, hi = np.searchsorted(t, [t[i] - 0.05, t[i] + 0.05])
    if speed[i:hi + 1].max() - speed[lo:i + 1].min() < 6:
        continue                                 # not a kick
    strike = lo + np.argmax(acc[lo:i + 1])       # peak acceleration

# One kick: s is the time from the start of the test, 0.02 s after the strike
s = t - (t[strike] + 0.02)
w = (s >= 0) & (s <= 0.38)
if (pos[w, 2].max() < 0.5 or pos[w, 2].min() <= 0
        or np.abs(np.diff(speed[w])).max() >= 2):
    skip()                                       # rolling, touched or bounced
near = np.abs(s) <= 0.01
r0 = np.array([np.polyval(np.polyfit(s[near], pos[near, i], 2), 0) for i in range(3)])
p1 = np.array([np.interp(0.10, s, pos[:, i]) for i in range(3)])
u = (p1 - r0)[:2] / np.linalg.norm((p1 - r0)[:2])  # horizontal direction
left = np.array([-u[1], u[0]])
X = (pos[:, :2] - r0[:2]) @ u
Y = (pos[:, :2] - r0[:2]) @ left
D = np.interp(0.38, s, X)
if D < 5:
    skip()                                       # lob or bounce
frame = np.column_stack([X - D + 11, Y, pos[:, 2]])   # plane at x = 11
start = np.array([11 - D, 0, r0[2]])
obs = [[np.interp(si, s, frame[:, i]) for i in range(3)] for si in (0.05, 0.10)]
truth = [np.interp(0.38, s, frame[:, i]) for i in (1, 2)]
\end{lstlisting}
