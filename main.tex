\section{Introduction}
% =====================================================================

In a penalty kick, one player shoots at a goal defended only by the
goalkeeper. The ball starts on a mark \SI{11}{\metre} in front of a goal
\SI{7.32}{\metre} wide and \SI{2.44}{\metre} high \citep{ifab2025}.
A well-struck penalty covers that distance in about \SI{0.4}{\second}, too
fast for the goalkeeper to wait and see where the ball goes. To block the
kick, the goalkeeper has to anticipate the trajectory from the first
instants of the ball's flight. This paper asks how well that can be done:
given the ball's position at two moments shortly after the kick, where will
it most likely cross the goal line, and how sure can we be?

The difficulty is that the first instants say little about the forces that
decide where the ball ends up. Over the first \SI{0.1}{\second} the ball
travels about \SI{3}{\metre}, and the curve that spin puts into its path
is only about \SI{1}{\centi\metre}, the same size as the precision of the
positions we are given. By the time the ball reaches the goal, the same spin
has moved it by tens of centimetres. Drag, which slows the ball, depends on
its speed and spin in ways that cannot be measured from two positions.
Predicting the crossing point is therefore not a curve-fitting problem but
an extrapolation problem, and an honest answer has to say how uncertain the
extrapolation is.

Our approach combines a physical model of the ball's flight with a
statistical treatment of what the observations cannot determine.
Section~\ref{sec:restatement} restates the problem and our interpretation
of it. Section~\ref{sec:simple-model} introduces the forces on the ball and
the assumptions of a simplified model. Section~\ref{sec:exact-fits} applies
that model in the case where the problem is well constrained, and finds the
trajectories that pass through the given positions to within their rounding.
Section~\ref{sec:montecarlo} extends this to a Monte Carlo method that samples
the quantities the data cannot fix, from distributions based on measured kicks,
and weights each sample by how well it explains the observations.
Section~\ref{sec:results} gives the predicted crossing point and its
uncertainty for the given scenario, together with tests of the method on
simulated kicks, and Section~\ref{sec:discussion} discusses the strengths
and weaknesses of the approach.

\section{Problem Restatement}
\label{sec:restatement}
% =====================================================================

A penalty kick is taken from the penalty mark. We use the coordinate system
of the problem, in metres:
\begin{itemize}
  \item the origin is at the penalty mark;
  \item the $x$-axis points toward the goal, which lies in the plane $x = 11$;
  \item the $y$-axis runs across the goal, with $+y$ on the kicker's left;
  \item the $z$-axis points up.
\end{itemize}
The goal mouth is the region $-3.66 < y < 3.66$, $0 < z < 2.44$ of the
plane $x = 11$. At $t = 0$ the centre of the ball is at
$\bm{r}_0 = (0, 0, 0.11)$, one ball radius above the mark. The position of
the ball is then observed at $t_1 = \SI{0.05}{\second}$ and
$t_2 = \SI{0.10}{\second}$.

The problem asks us to develop a general method that, given the coordinates
of the ball at $t_1$ and $t_2$,
\begin{enumerate}
  \item predicts the point at which the ball is most likely to enter the goal, and
  \item estimates the uncertainty of that prediction.
\end{enumerate}
We are to test the method and apply it to the scenario in
Table~\ref{tab:scenario}.

\begin{table}[h]
  \centering
  \caption{Observed positions of the ball's centre in the given scenario.
    The position at $t = 0$ is given exactly.}
  \label{tab:scenario}
  \begin{tabular}{S[table-format=1.2] S[table-format=1.2] S[table-format=1.2] S[table-format=1.2]}
    \toprule
    {$t$ (\si{\second})} & {$x$ (\si{\metre})} & {$y$ (\si{\metre})} & {$z$ (\si{\metre})} \\
    \midrule
    0.00 & 0.00 & 0.00 & 0.11 \\
    0.05 & 1.50 & 0.45 & 0.44 \\
    0.10 & 2.96 & 0.86 & 0.74 \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
\end{table}

We interpret the problem as follows.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{A general method.} The method takes any two observed
    positions as input. Nothing in it is tuned to the given scenario,
    which serves as one application of it.
  \item \textbf{Coordinates of the ball.} The problem gives the position
    at $t = 0$ as the centre of the ball, so we take the observed
    coordinates at $t_1$ and $t_2$ to be positions of the centre as well.
  \item \textbf{Entering the goal.} We take the point where the ball
    ``enters the goal'' to be the $(y, z)$ position of its centre when the
    centre reaches the goal plane $x = 11$. A shot counts as on target when
    the centre crosses inside the goal mouth defined above, as \emph{wide}
    when $|y| \geq 3.66$, and as \emph{over} when $z \geq 2.44$.
  \item \textbf{Off-target shots are part of the answer.} The ball is not
    guaranteed to enter the goal, so we predict where it crosses the whole
    goal plane, inside or outside the frame. Alongside the most likely
    point we report the probability that the ball enters the goal, and the
    probabilities that it goes wide or over.
  \item \textbf{Precision of the data.} The observed positions are given
    to the nearest centimetre, so each coordinate is known only to within
    about $\pm\SI{5}{\milli\metre}$. A trajectory that passes within this
    distance of every observation is consistent with the data.
  \item \textbf{Most likely point and uncertainty.} Because the observations
    cannot determine every quantity that affects the flight, the prediction
    is a probability distribution over the goal plane. We take the most
    likely crossing point to be the peak of that distribution, and describe
    the uncertainty by its spread, by the regions that contain 68\% and 95\%
    of the probability, and by the outcome probabilities.
  \item \textbf{Testing.} We test the method both on the given scenario and
    on simulated kicks whose true crossing points are known, which checks
    whether the stated uncertainty is honest.
\end{itemize}
